First-Principles Treatise · Target Fixed Points ($\pi^*$) vs. Approximation Paths

RL Algorithms for the Mathematically Inclined

A Two-Layer Coordinate System for Post-Training—Separating What Moves the Target $\pi^*$ (Layer A) from What Stabilizes the Off-Policy Path (Layers B–D), Audited Across 105 Primary Papers

Abstract. Flat five-axis taxonomies can index which line of code a post-training paper modified, but they miss the fundamental structural hierarchy of reinforcement learning: which choices determine where the policy converges (the target distribution or optimal solution set $\pi^*$), and which choices determine how it gets there (bias, variance, velocity $B$, and stability)? Starting from the regularized optimum $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R(x,y)/\beta)$ and the canonical gradient $g = \sum_x w(p_x)\sum_{i,t} c_{i,t}\,\psi(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t})\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$, this treatise replaces flat indexing with a falsifiable Two-Layer Coordinate System conditioned on base-model support ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$) and compute: Layer A (Target Layer: Signal A1, Prompt Weighting A2, Length Weighting A3, Regularization A4) sets the target $\pi^*$ (or cross-prompt objective trade-off) and asymptotic ceiling $A$; Layer B (Operating Regime B1: off-policy reuse $\mu$, async lag $\tau$, engine/MoE drift) determines whether update control is active or algebraically empty ($r \equiv 1 \implies \texttt{C1} = \varnothing$); Layer C (Approximation Layer: 4-Quadrant Ratio Processing C1, Credit/Baseline C2, Effective KL Step C3) governs convergence velocity and off-policy stability (and selects among optimal modes on the $\beta=0$ simplex face); and Layer D (Derived Entropy & Yield Dynamics) marks the phase boundary where approximation instabilities collapse reachable support. We test this coordinate system against controlled same-cell, cross-cell, and contradictory experiments across 105 papers, and conclude with the five frontier production battlegrounds that lie beyond single-benchmark RLVR.

Preface: Navigating 10,000 Post-Training Papers via the Two-Layer Fixed-Point Coordinate System ($\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{C} \to \mathbf{D}$)

Between classical policy gradients (REINFORCE, PPO), offline and online preference optimization (DPO, IPO, SimPO, KTO, ORPO, Online DPO), critic-free group reinforcement learning (RLOO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO, DAPO, MaxRL), off-policy and Mixture-of-Experts stabilizers (CISPO, GSPO, GMPO, SAPO, TOPR, IcePop, Routing Replay R3), step-level process rewards (PRMs, VinePPO, PRIME, PURE), multi-turn agentic RL (Search-R1, GiGPO), self-play games (SPPO, Nash-MD), and On-Policy Distillation (GKD, MiniLLM, OPD, DeepSeek-V4), the LLM post-training literature has grown to more than 10,000 papers.

When indexing what a paper modifies in a training codebase, it is natural to list five operational modules: Objective (what distribution or equilibrium is targeted), Signal (where rewards come from), Data (how rollouts are sampled and filtered), Estimator (how rewards turn into gradients), and Update Control (clipping, importance ratios, and steps per batch). While a flat five-module list is useful for indexing which line of code a paper touched, treating those five dimensions as parallel peers throws away the single most important causal structure in reinforcement learning: which choices determine where the policy converges (the stationary fixed point $\pi^*$), and which choices only determine how it gets there (bias, variance, velocity, and stability)?

1. The Fundamental Split: Target Layer ($\pi^*$) vs. Approximation Layer

Return to the closed-form solution of KL-regularized reinforcement learning (Chapter 1.1):

$$\pi^*(y\mid x) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{R(x, y)}{\beta}\Big)$$
  • The Target Layer ($\mathbf{A}$: Determines where the fixed point or optimal set $\pi^*$ is): Comprises the supervision signal and its error structure (A1), the prompt-level utility weighting $F(p_x)$ (A2), the response-length weighting $c_{i,t}$ (A3), and the regularizer $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}$ (A4)—acting on top of the given Base Model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (which fixes the initial support $\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$). Layer $\mathbf{A}$ determines the asymptotic ceiling $A$ and dictates how the objective can be reward-hacked.
  • The Operating Regime ($\mathbf{B}$) & Approximation Layer ($\mathbf{C}$: Determines whether and how fast we reach $\pi^*$—and selects among modes when $\beta=0$): Comprises the off-policy regime ($\mu$ steps/batch, async lag $\tau$, hybrid-engine/MoE drift $\delta_{\text{sys}}$ in B1), the 4-quadrant importance-ratio gate $\psi(r, \hat{A})$ (C1, which is algebraically empty $\varnothing$ when B1 is strictly on-policy $r\equiv 1$), the credit/baseline estimator (C2), and the effective KL step size (C3). Note a crucial mathematical subtlety: when $\beta > 0$, $\pi^*$ is a unique interior distribution determined solely by Layer $\mathbf{A}$; when $\beta = 0$ and $R(x,y)\in\{0,1\}$ (standard modern RLVR), $\pi^*$ degenerates into the entire simplex face $\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^+(x))$ of distributions supported on correct responses ($p_x = 1$), so Layer $\mathbf{C}$'s implicit mirror-descent trajectory from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ selects which mode on that optimal simplex survives.
  • The Dynamical Phase Boundary ($\mathbf{D}$: Entropy Drift & Support Reachability): Because online RL samples from $\pi_k$ itself, Layer $\mathbf{C}$ exhibits a piecewise regime: inside the stable dynamical interior, Layer $\mathbf{C}$ affects only variance and convergence velocity $B$; however, if Layer $\mathbf{C}$ crosses a dynamical collapse threshold (e.g., Q-IV negative-logit explosion on shared prefixes, un-replayed MoE routing drift, or length penalties under $L_{\text{cap}} < T^*(x)$), it triggers irreversible entropy/support collapse ($\mathcal{H}(\pi_k) \to 0$, D1), directly lowering the realized ceiling $A$.
The Master Fixed-Point Diagnostic. Whenever a paper proposes a new post-training modification, the decisive question is not "Which module did it edit?" but:
"Did this modification alter the Target Layer $\mathbf{A}$ ($\pi^*$ or cross-prompt weighting $F(p_x)$), or did it only alter the Approximation Layer $\mathbf{C}$?"
  • If Layer A is unchanged: It is an efficiency, variance, or off-policy stability intervention. Inside the stable dynamical interior, its gain appears in convergence speed $B$ or high-$\mu$ throughput—unless it prevents a phase-boundary collapse in D1.
  • If Layer A is changed: It is a Target-Layer modification—very often disguised as an estimator or update-control trick (Section 2.3):
    • GRPO dividing by group std $\sigma_G$ looks like estimator variance normalization, but actually changes A2 from $\sum_x p_x$ to $\sum_x 2\arcsin\sqrt{p_x}$ (re-allocating cross-prompt gradient flow toward $p_x \to 1$ and $p_x \to 0$ extremes).
    • Per-sequence $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ vs. token-mean aggregation looks like an estimator reduction detail, but actually changes A3 by reweighting trajectories by length $|y_i|^{-1}$.
    • Putting the $k_3$ KL estimator directly inside the differentiated loss looks like a regularizer implementation detail, but has a biased gradient that shifts A4 away from the reverse-KL fixed point $\pi^*$.
    • Dynamic sampling (dropping $p_x \in \{0, 1\}$ groups) looks like a data distribution change, but leaves the target direction unchanged because $p_x \in \{0, 1\}$ groups had zero expected gradient anyway!

2. A Two-Layer Coordinate System with Two Falsifiable Predictive Rules

By reading coordinates directly from the canonical update equation (Section 2.3)

$$g(\theta) = \sum_x \underbrace{w(p_x)}_{\text{A2}} \sum_{i,t} \underbrace{c_{i,t}}_{\text{A3}}\,\underbrace{\psi(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t})}_{\text{C1, C2 (iff B1 }\ne\text{ on-policy)}}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}) - \underbrace{\beta\,\nabla_\theta\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}})}_{\text{A4}}$$

and merging sub-variants whose controlled differences fall inside the evaluation Minimum Detectable Effect ($\text{MDE} \approx \pm 4.6$ points on single-seed AIME30 at $k=32$), our coordinate table yields two piecewise, falsifiable predictions (audited across 105 primary papers in Section 2.3):

  1. Rule 1 (Same Target $\mathbf{A} \implies$ Same Ceiling $A$): Under matched boundary conditions Cond ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$, prompt difficulty distribution, and compute budget) and absence of early entropy collapse (D1), any two algorithms with the same Target Layer coordinates (A1, A2, A3, A4) reach the same asymptotic ceiling within the noise floor (e.g., 3-seed `VC-PPO` $50.9$ vs. `GRPO` $50.0$, and single-run `GRPO` $50.2$, `RLOO` $49.9$, `VC-PPO` $50.4$ in GenAC; single-run `Semi-Online DPO` $58.9$ and `Online DPO` $58.7$ vs. `GRPO` $58.1$ on MATH-500 in 2506.21495).
  2. Rule 2 (Same $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}) \implies$ Same Training Curve): When operating regime B1 and approximation coordinates (C1, C2, C3) also match (with effective KL step size C3 tuned to the same scale), the entire training trajectory coincides within noise (e.g., `DPO`, `IPO`, `SimPO`, and `ORPO` within $\sim 1.5$ AlpacaEval LC points once $\beta$ and LR are swept in 2502.01237; on-policy token-level PG and off-policy token-level clipping with routing replay `R2/R3` stabilizing Qwen3-30B-A3B without sequence-level `GSPO` in 2512.01374, and `GSPO`, `TIS`, `IcePop`, `FP16`, and `CIS` landing within $\sim 1.6$ points across 3 MoE models $\times$ 3 seeds in 2609.32444).

Part I · Variational Foundations, Emergence & The Unified Spectrum

Deriving the exact target distribution of RL post-training, resolving the paradox of support sharpening vs. compositional emergence in parameter space, and unifying SFT, DPO, RLVR, PRMs, and On-Policy Distillation along two information-theoretic coordinates.

1. Variational Foundations: Support Conservation, Compositional Emergence & The Post-Training Spectrum

Every post-training algorithm for an autoregressive language model $\pi_\theta(y_{1:T} \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^T \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$ is an attempt to solve a single variational problem under finite samples and finite compute. Understanding that variational problem—and where its tabular approximation breaks down in neural parameter space—immediately resolves the deepest debates in the post-training literature.

1.1 The Three-Layer Variational Reduction

Layer 1 (The Functional Objective). Given a prompt distribution $\mathcal{D}$, a black-box reward or verifier $R(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}$, and a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, we seek a policy $\pi$ that maximizes expected reward subject to a reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence budget:

$$\max_{\pi}\; \mathcal{J}_{\beta}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[R(x, y)\big] - \beta\,D_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big)\right]$$

Why does RL differ fundamentally from supervised fine-tuning (SFT)? First, the sampling measure $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ depends on the parameters $\theta$: optimization moves the very distribution from which training data is drawn. Second, $R(x, y)$ is non-differentiable with respect to discrete tokens $y_{1:T}$. Differentiating $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(x, y)]$ therefore requires the score-function (log-derivative) identity $\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(x, y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\!\left[\big(R(x, y) - b(x)\big)\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right]$$

where any prompt-only baseline $b(x)$ has zero expectation under $\pi_\theta$ because $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)] = \nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$.

Layer 2 (The Closed-Form Gibbs Target & Implicit Mirror-Descent Duality). Because $\mathcal{J}_\beta(\pi)$ is strictly concave in $\pi(\cdot \mid x)$ on the probability simplex, setting its functional Euler–Lagrange derivative $\frac{\delta \mathcal{J}_\beta}{\delta \pi(y\mid x)} = R(x,y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} - \beta - \lambda(x) = 0$ yields the unique global optimizer:

$$\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{R(x, y)}{\beta}\right), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y' \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{R(x, y')}{\beta}\right)$$

This is exponential tilting (Gibbs reweighting) of the reference prior $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Even when modern verifiable-reward RL (RLVR) sets the explicit KL penalty coefficient to $\beta = 0$ (Chapter 7.3), this equation still governs the trajectory of training. Why? Because policy gradient with step size $\eta$ performs entropic mirror descent starting from $\pi_0 = \pi_{\text{ref}}$:

$$\pi_{k+1}(y \mid x) \;\propto\; \pi_k(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\eta\,\hat{A}_k(x, y)\big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_K(y \mid x) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\eta \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \hat{A}_k(x, y)\right)$$

Under stationary advantages, $K$ mirror-descent updates of step size $\eta$ trace the exact exponential-tilting family with an implicit temperature $\beta_{\text{eff}} \propto (\eta K)^{-1}$. Removing the explicit KL anchor simply lets $\beta_{\text{eff}} \to 0$ as $K$ grows—proving why an explicit KL penalty to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is mathematically dual to early stopping along the reward–KL Pareto frontier (Gao et al., 2210.10760). Three immediate laws follow from the Gibbs solution $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R/\beta)$:

  1. Function-space support conservation ($\operatorname{supp}(\pi^*) \subseteq \operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$). Wherever $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) = 0$, multiplying by $\exp(R(x,y)/\beta)$ still yields strictly zero (`y9` in Figure 1 below). In non-parametric function space on a single prompt, RL reweights existing probability mass; it cannot create support ex nihilo.
  2. Mode sharpening as $\beta_{\text{eff}} \to 0$. As $\beta_{\text{eff}}$ shrinks, mass drains exponentially from suboptimal completions onto the highest-reward modes inside $\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$, raising single-sample accuracy ($\text{pass@1}$) while contracting distribution entropy and large-$k$ sample diversity.
  3. Exponential proxy amplification (Goodhart's Law). Whenever the reward function $R(x, y)$ assigns a false positive or inflated score to a degenerate sequence (`y7` in Figure 1) that also enjoys non-negligible prior mass $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_7 \mid x)$, driving $\beta_{\text{eff}} \to 0$ concentrates $100\%$ of the asymptotic probability mass onto the hack. Reward hacking is not an optimization bug; it is the exact analytical optimum of $\mathcal{J}_\beta$ under an imperfect proxy $R$.

1.2 Law I · Resolving the Emergence Paradox: Function-Space Sharpening vs. Parameter-Space Compositional Transfer

A central controversy in the 2025–2026 literature pits two observations against each other. On one hand, controlled pass@$k$ studies show that RL post-training raises $\text{pass@1}$ while leaving large-$k$ coverage $\text{pass@}k$ ($k \in [128, 1024]$) flat or lower than the base model (Yue et al., 2504.13837; The Invisible Leash, 2507.14843). On the other hand, long-horizon RL runs (such as DeepSeek-R1-Zero or ProRL; 2505.24864) clearly solve complex multi-step reasoning problems where the base model's empirical pass@128 was zero. How can both be true?

First-principles analysis resolves this immediately by distinguishing sequence-level tabular tilting from token-level parameter-coupled dynamics:

  1. Why single-prompt tabular updates only sharpen: If $\pi(y \mid x)$ were parameterized as an independent lookup table for each full trajectory $y$ on prompt $x$, then any trajectory $y^*$ not sampled within the finite rollout budget $G$ receives zero positive gradient. Worse, because probability mass on the simplex sums to $1$, reinforcing the sampled correct trajectories drains mass from unsampled trajectories, causing $\text{pass@}k$ at large $k$ to contract toward the subset of modes hit early in training.
  2. How shared parameters $\theta$ create exponential compositional lift across prompts: An LLM is not a trajectory lookup table; it is a parameter-shared Markov transition kernel $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)$ where $s_t = (x, y_{<t})$. Under a gradient step $\Delta \theta = \eta \sum_{i, t} \hat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})$, the first-order change in log-probability for a trajectory $y'_{1:T'}$ on a different, harder prompt $x'$ is governed by the neural tangent (feature) kernel $\Theta_\theta(s, s') = \langle \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s), \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y'_{t'} \mid s') \rangle$:
    $$\Delta \log \pi_\theta(y'_{1:T'} \mid x') = \eta \sum_{t'=1}^{T'} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \hat{A}_{i,t}\,\Theta_\theta\!\big((x', y'_{<t'}),\; (x_i, y_{i,<t})\big) + \mathcal{O}(\eta^2)$$
    Suppose solving a hard problem $x'$ requires chaining $m$ intermediate cognitive transitions (e.g., subgoal decomposition, self-verification, backtracking `"Wait, let me re-check..."`), each having a small prior transition probability $p_{\text{step}}$ under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Prior to RL, the joint probability of successfully chaining all $m$ verification steps is $P_{\text{ref}}(y^* \mid x') \propto p_{\text{step}}^m$—which for $p_{\text{step}} = 0.2$ and $m = 10$ is $10^{-7}$, completely invisible to $\text{pass@1024}$! However, if those same atomic verification transitions occur and succeed on easier prompts $x_i$ ($m = 1\text{ or }2$, where $p_{\text{step}} = 0.2$ is easily sampled within $G = 16$ rollouts), positive RL updates on $x_i$ increase the shared transition probability from $p_{\text{step}}$ to $p_{\text{step}} + \delta$. On the hard prompt $x'$, the trajectory probability is multiplied by:
    $$\frac{\pi_\theta(y^* \mid x')}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^* \mid x')} \;\approx\; \left(1 + \frac{\delta}{p_{\text{step}}}\right)^m \;\approx\; \exp\!\left(m\,\frac{\delta}{p_{\text{step}}}\right)$$
    Lifting an atomic verification transition from $p_{\text{step}} = 0.20$ to $0.50$ across $m = 10$ compositional steps raises the full trajectory's probability by $(2.5)^{10} \approx 9{,}500\times$, moving it from $10^{-7}$ (unreachable) into the $10^{-3}$ regime where on-policy rollouts on $x'$ can finally sample and reinforce it directly!

Theorem 1 (Necessary & Sufficient Conditions for Capability Expansion vs. Pure Sharpening). Outcome-reward RL expands effective reasoning coverage beyond empirical $\text{pass@}k(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ if and only if three conditions hold simultaneously:

  1. Atomic motif support: The base model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already assigns non-trivial probability $p_{\text{step}} > 0$ to atomic verification, decomposition, and backtracking transitions on solvable bridge prompts (Gandhi et al., 2503.01307).
  2. Bridge curriculum density: The training set contains intermediate-difficulty prompts ($p_x \in (0.05, 0.80)$) where those atomic transitions correlate with higher binary reward $R(x, y)$.
  3. Entropy survival: Token-level policy entropy $\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t))$ is prevented from collapsing prematurely onto superficial formatting or majority-vote modes before cross-prompt kernel transfer $\Theta_\theta$ amplifies the multi-step compositions.

When Condition 1 or 3 fails, RL degenerates into pure distribution sharpening, bounded above by the base model's majority-vote accuracy:

$$\text{pass@1}_{\text{pure-sharpening}}(\pi_\theta) \;\le\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\mathbf{1}\big\{\arg\max_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \in \mathcal{Y}^*(x)\big\}\Big] = \operatorname{maj@\infty}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$$

How Theorem 1 denoises the "Sharpening vs. Expansion" and "Base Model Ceiling" literature:

  • Why pass@$k$ shrinks on static single-difficulty runs: On Omni-MATH (Yue et al., 2504.13837), standard GRPO raised pass@1 from $9.9\%$ to $42.5\%$ while pass@256 fell from $67.2\%$ to $64.3\%$, and on AIME-24 at $k=1{,}024$ the RL-solved set was a strict subset of the base model's. Similarly, tracking individual completions showed $163$ solutions leaving support versus $36$ entering (2507.14843). In contrast, when ProRL trained for $2{,}000+$ steps with explicit KL/optimizer resets and curriculum progression, Reasoning Gym accuracy rose from $4.24\%$ to $59.06\%$, unlocking multi-step compositions that were empirically $0/128$ under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (2505.24864; 2610.04158).
  • Why priming atomic behaviors before RL dwarfs changing the RL loss: Llama-3.2-3B stalls under RL on Countdown because its pre-RL support lacks verification and backtracking motifs (Condition 1 fails). Fine-tuning Llama-3.2-3B on a small set of trajectories containing verification and backtracking motifs—even when those priming trajectories end in incorrect final answers—causes subsequent RL to match Qwen-2.5-3B's steep trajectory (Gandhi et al., 2503.01307) B. Likewise, continuing pre-training on 220B math-rich tokens (OctoThinker, 2506.20512) closes the cross-family RL gap without touching the RL algorithm.

  1. Pre-existing high-accuracy modal strategy: Prior to RL, $65\%$ of Qwen2.5-Math-7B rollouts use Python code-reasoning ($60.9\%$ accuracy) while $35\%$ use prose ($28.0\%$ accuracy). First-principles takeaway: Any gain within the $\operatorname{maj@}k(\pi_{\text{ref}}) - \text{pass@1}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ gap can be achieved by blind sharpening (including one-shot RLVR, 2504.20571, and label-free entropy minimization, 2505.15134). An RL algorithm only proves it extracts information from the reward to the extent that it beats a random-reward / format-only control on an uncontaminated base model.

1.3 Law II · The Unified Post-Training Spectrum (From SFT & DPO to RLVR & OPD)

Why has the post-training field fractured into SFT, Rejection Sampling (RFT/ReST), Offline Preference Optimization (DPO/IPO/SimPO), Online Preference Optimization, Outcome-Reward RL (RLVR/GRPO), Process Reward Models (PRMs), and On-Policy Distillation (OPD)? From first principles, every paradigm is uniquely specified by **three mathematical coordinates** in how it approximates $\pi^*$:

  1. Rollout Measure ($\mu$): Are training trajectories drawn from a static external distribution $\mu_{\text{off}}(y\mid x)$ (teacher or frozen dataset) or from the student's own live autoregressive distribution $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$?
  2. Supervision Channel Capacity ($\mathcal{C}$, in bits per token): Does the supervision signal provide $\mathcal{O}(\log|\mathcal{V}|) \approx 10\text{ bits}$ at every token $t$, or a single binary scalar $R(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$ with Shannon mutual information $I(R; Y\mid x) = H_{\text{Ber}}(p_x) \le 1\text{ bit}$ smeared across $T = 10^4$ tokens ($\le 10^{-4}\text{ bits/token}$)?

2. Problem-Driven Algorithm Evolution, the Canonical Estimator & the Two-Layer Sufficiency Coordinate System ($\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{C} \to \mathbf{D}$)

In this chapter, we first derive the core algorithms of LLM reinforcement learning from a problem-solving perspective—showing the exact mathematical bottleneck each algorithm solved and the new bottleneck it created (Section 2.1). We then factorize their equations into a single canonical estimator and prove the $r \equiv 1$ On-Policy Collapse Theorem (Section 2.2). Finally, we organize the entire post-training design space into the Two-Layer Sufficiency Coordinate System ($\text{Target }\mathbf{A} \times \text{Regime }\mathbf{B} \times \text{Approximation }\mathbf{C} \to \text{Dynamics }\mathbf{D}$), dissect off-policy and credit choices via $2\times 2$ Quadrant Maps, and verify the two predictive rules against controlled same-cell and cross-cell experiments across 105 papers (Section 2.3).

2.1 From Problems to Algorithms: The Five-Stage Constructive Evolution

Stage 1 · REINFORCE (1992): Solving Non-Differentiable Discrete Rewards

The Problem: We want to maximize $\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[R(x, y)]$, where $y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$ is a discrete token sequence sampled from an autoregressive transformer and $R(x, y)$ is a non-differentiable black-box verifier or reward model.

The Algorithmic Solution: Apply the score-function identity $\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and factorize $\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})$:

$$\hat{g}_{\text{REINFORCE}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} R(x, y_i) \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}), \qquad y_i \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$$

What Breaks in Practice: (1) Sign ambiguity & $\mathcal{O}(T^2)$ variance: if $R(x, y) \in [0, 1]$, bad trajectories get zero gradient instead of negative suppression, and summing uncentered score functions across $T = 10{,}000$ tokens causes variance to explode as $\mathcal{O}(T^2)$. (2) Single-step sample waste ($\mu = 1$): because the expectation is under $\pi_\theta$, taking a single optimizer step invalidates the batch $\{y_i\}$, wasting $90\%+$ of GPU time on autoregressive generation.

Stage 2 · PPO-RLHF (2017 / InstructGPT 2022): Solving Sample Reuse ($\mu > 1$) & Token Credit

The Problem: How can we reuse a generated batch of rollouts $\{y_i\} \sim \pi_{\text{old}}$ for $\mu > 1$ gradient steps without catastrophic off-policy divergence, while localizing credit across $T$ tokens?

The Algorithmic Solution: PPO introduces (a) a learned token-level value critic $V_\phi(s_t)$ with Generalized Advantage Estimation $\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}$, and (b) the per-token importance ratio $r_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}$ inside a pessimistic clipped surrogate:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_{i,t}\!\Big[\min\!\big(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\text{GAE}},\; \operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\text{GAE}}\big)\Big], \qquad r_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})}$$

What Breaks at Reasoning Scale: (1) The 4-model memory wall: maintaining Actor $\pi_\theta$, Critic $V_\phi$, Reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and Reward Model $R_{\text{RM}}$ simultaneously doubles memory and adds $1.6\times$ compute overhead per step. (2) Long-CoT GAE attenuation: when a binary outcome reward $R \in \{0, 1\}$ arrives only at terminal token $T = 8{,}000$, standard $\lambda = 0.95$ GAE attenuates the reward signal at token $t$ by $\lambda^{T-t}$ ($0.95^{200} \approx 3\times 10^{-5}$), leaving the first $7{,}800$ tokens blind to whether the final answer was right or wrong (Chapter 4.1).

Stage 3 · RLOO & GRPO (2024 / DeepSeekMath & DeepSeek-R1): Eliminating the Value Critic

The Problem: In verifiable reasoning (math, code, logic), we only care about sequence-level outcome reward $R(x, y_i) \in \{0, 1\}$. Can we eliminate the learned critic $V_\phi$ entirely by estimating the baseline $b(x)$ directly from a group of $G$ parallel rollouts $\{y_1, \dots, y_G\} \sim \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$ on the same prompt?

The Algorithmic Solutions:

  • RLOO (Leave-One-Out REINFORCE; Ahmadian et al., 2402.14740): Subtracts the average reward of the other $G-1$ rollouts on prompt $x$, yielding an exact unbiased sequence advantage: $$\hat{A}_i^{\text{RLOO}} = R_i - \frac{1}{G - 1}\sum_{j \ne i} R_j = \frac{G}{G - 1}\big(R_i - \bar{R}_G\big), \qquad \text{where } \bar{R}_G = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G R_j$$
  • GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2402.03300; DeepSeek-R1, 2501.12948): Embeds the group baseline inside PPO's clipped objective for $\mu > 1$ multi-epoch updates, but adds three extra design choices: (1) divides the centered reward by the group standard deviation $\sigma_G = \operatorname{std}(\{R_1,\dots,R_G\})$, (2) averages the token loss within each response by $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$, and (3) adds a per-token $k_3$ KL penalty directly to the loss:
    $$\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \Big[\min\!\big(r_{i,t}\hat{A}_i^{\text{GRPO}},\, \operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t}, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}_i^{\text{GRPO}}\big) - \beta\,k_3(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}})\Big], \qquad \hat{A}_i^{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{R_i - \bar{R}_G}{\sigma_G + \epsilon}$$

What Breaks When Scaling GRPO to Long-CoT Reasoning: Scaling GRPO to $16\text{k–}64\text{k}$ reasoning traces at $\mu = 4\text{–}16$ updates per batch exposed four mathematical pathologies inside $\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}$:

  1. Pathology 1 (Objective & Length Distortion $\to$ Target Layer A2, A3): Dividing by $\sigma_G = \sqrt{p_x(1-p_x)}$ gives a $3\times$ gradient boost not only to hard prompts ($p_x \to 0$) but also to trivial, nearly solved prompts ($p_x \to 1$)! Simultaneously, dividing each trajectory by its own length $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ penalizes long wrong answers ($R_i = 0 \implies \hat{A}_i < 0$) less per token than short wrong answers, causing incorrect chains of thought to bloat toward $L_{\max}$.
  2. Pathology 2 (Rare-Token Clipping Starvation & Entropy Collapse $\to$ Approximation C1 & Dynamics D1): Under symmetric $\varepsilon = 0.20$, a rare reasoning pivot token (`"Wait"`, `"Alternatively"`) with $\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}) = 10^{-3}$ hits the upper clip wall $r_{i,t} = 1.20$ as soon as its probability rises by $0.0002$ on micro-step 1, getting zero-masked for the remaining $\mu - 1$ steps—while high-probability mode tokens ($\pi_{\text{old}} = 0.50$) can rise by $0.10$, collapsing policy entropy $\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta) \to 0$.
  3. Pathology 3 (Zero-Variance Dead Groups $\to$ Dynamics D2): Whenever all $G$ rollouts on prompt $x$ fail ($\sum R_i = 0$) or succeed ($\sum R_i = G$), $\hat{A}_i^{\text{GRPO}} \equiv 0$. These $0$-bit groups contribute zero gradient signal while shrinking the effective batch size.
  4. Pathology 4 (Negative-Quadrant & Hybrid-Engine Explosion $\to$ Regime B1 & Ratio Control C1): Over $T = 16{,}000$ tokens at $\mu = 4\text{–}16$ steps/batch—especially on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models where vLLM inference and Megatron training route tokens to different experts—negative-advantage tokens ($\hat{A}_i < 0$) suffer unbounded logit drift, crashing training mid-run.

Stage 4 · The 2025–2026 Surgical Fixes: Dr. GRPO, DAPO, MaxRL, CISPO, GSPO & R3

Every major RLVR algorithm of 2025–2026 targets one or more of GRPO's four pathologies:

  • Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2503.20783) — Fixing Pathology 1: Drops $\div\sigma_G$ ($\hat{A}_i = R_i - \bar{R}_G \equiv \frac{G-1}{G}\hat{A}_i^{\text{RLOO}}$) and replaces $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ with a constant token normalizer $\frac{1}{M}$, restoring the exact unbiased sequence policy gradient.
  • MaxRL (2602.02710) & Pass@$k$ Training (2508.10751) — Fixing Pathology 1: Replaces GRPO's U-shaped weight $1/\sqrt{p_x(1-p_x)}$ with monotone decreasing weights $w(p_x) = \frac{1-(1-p_x)^T}{p_x} \to \frac{1}{p_x}$ (ascending $\sum_x \log p_x$) or $k(1-p_x)^{k-1}$ (ascending $\text{pass@}k$), up-weighting hard prompts ($p_x \to 0$) without amplifying easy ones ($p_x \to 1$).
  • DAPO (Yu et al., 2503.14476) — Fixing Pathologies 1, 2 & 3: Replaces symmetric clipping with Clip-Higher ($\varepsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.20,\; \varepsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$); filters out $\sum R_i \in \{0, G\}$ dead groups via Dynamic Sampling; switches to batch token-mean normalization $\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^G |y_j|}$; and drops the explicit KL penalty ($\beta = 0$).
  • CISPO (MiniMax-M1, 2506.13585) — Fixing Pathology 2 at High Batch Reuse ($\mu = 16$): Replaces upper zero-masking with a stop-gradient capped importance weight $\operatorname{sg}\!\big(\min(r_{i,t}, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}})\big)$ so rare reasoning tokens continue learning across all $\mu = 16$ micro-steps.
  • GSPO (2507.18071), TOPR (2503.14286), IcePop (2510.18855) & R3 (2510.11370) — Fixing Pathology 4: GSPO replaces noisy token ratios $r_{i,t}$ with the sequence geometric mean $s_i = (\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} r_{i,t})^{1/|y_i|}$; TOPR and Dual-Clip bound negative-advantage ($\hat{A}_i < 0$) ratios; IcePop masks tokens where the sampler-to-trainer ratio $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{train}}/\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}$ exits $[0.5, 5.0]$; and R3 replays the inference engine's exact MoE expert assignments during backpropagation.

2.2 Factorizing the Evolution: The Master Estimator & the $r \equiv 1$ Collapse Theorem

Comparing the equations of Stage 1 through Stage 4 reveals that every policy-gradient algorithm in the literature computes the exact same master expression over a group of $G$ sampled trajectories $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$, differing only in three algebraic slots $(\hat{A}_{i,t},\; w_{i,t}\cdot m_{i,t},\; c_{i,t})$:

$$\hat{g}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{G} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \underbrace{c_{i,t}}_{\substack{\text{Slot 3: Aggregation}\\\text{(Dr.GRPO vs. GRPO/DAPO)}}} \cdot \underbrace{w_{i,t}(r_{i,t}) \cdot m_{i,t}(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t})}_{\substack{\text{Slot 2: Trust-Region Weight & Gate}\\\text{(PPO / DAPO / CISPO / GSPO / SAPO)}}} \cdot \underbrace{\hat{A}_{i,t}}_{\substack{\text{Slot 1: Advantage & Scale}\\\text{(GAE / RLOO / GRPO / MaxRL / OPD)}}} \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$$

Differentiating $\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})$ with respect to the pre-softmax logit vector $z_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$ gives the exact token-level force on vocabulary token $v \in \mathcal{V}$:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{\mathcal{J}}}{\partial z_{i,t,v}} = c_{i,t} \cdot w_{i,t}(r_{i,t}) \cdot m_{i,t}(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t}) \cdot \hat{A}_{i,t} \cdot \Big(\mathbf{1}\{v = y_{i,t}\} - \pi_\theta(v \mid s_{i,t})\Big)$$

The Three Policies and Two Physical Gaps in Every Update. Three policy evaluations participate in every distributed RL step: (1) $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}$, the rollout engine's policy (vLLM/SGLang, often FP8/BF16 with paged attention) that generated $y_i$; (2) $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{train}}$, the trainer's policy evaluated once at $\theta_{\text{old}}$ before micro-step 1; and (3) $\pi_\theta^{\text{train}}$, the trainer's current policy at micro-step $k \in \{1, \dots, \mu\}$. Standard surrogates see only the trainer-side ratio $r_{i,t}(\theta)$, whereas the true off-policy ratio factors across two physical gaps:

$$r_{i,t}^{\text{true}}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta^{\text{train}}(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})} = \underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{train}}(y_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}(y_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})}}_{\rho_{i,t}^{\text{roll}}\text{ (Gap 1: Engine Mismatch)}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\pi_\theta^{\text{train}}(y_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{train}}(y_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})}}_{r_{i,t}(\theta)\text{ (Gap 2: Policy Lag)}}$$

Proposition 1 (The $r \equiv 1$ On-Policy Collapse Identity). Suppose each rollout batch receives a single optimizer step ($\mu = 1$, zero asynchronous lag $\tau = 0$) and $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{train}}$ is evaluated on the trainer at current parameters $\theta$. Then at the point of differentiation, $r_{i,t}(\theta) \equiv 1$ for every token $(i, t)$, and PPO-clip, GRPO, DAPO (Clip-Higher), Dual-Clip, CISPO, SAPO, TOPR, GSPO, and GMPO all return the exact same gradient once $\hat{A}_{i,t}$, $c_{i,t}$, and $\beta$ are matched:

$$\left.\hat{g}(\theta)\right|_{r_{i,t} \equiv 1} = \sum_{i=1}^{G} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} c_{i,t} \, \hat{A}_{i,t} \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$$

Proof. At $r_{i,t} = 1$: $1 \in [1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}}, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}]$ so $m_{i,t} = 1$ and $w_{i,t}(1) = 1$; $\min(1, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}) = 1$ (CISPO); $4\sigma(0)(1-\sigma(0)) = 1$ (SAPO); and $s_i = 1^{1/|y_i|} = 1$ with $\left.\nabla_\theta s_i\right|_{r=1} = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})$ (GSPO/GMPO). Note that if an explicit $k_3$ KL penalty is added outside the surrogate ($\beta > 0$ in original GRPO vs. $\beta = 0$ in DAPO/GSPO), its gradient $-\beta\big(\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta} - 1\big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ does not vanish at $r \equiv 1$ once $\pi_\theta \ne \pi_{\text{ref}}$. $\blacksquare$

Crucial Empirical Corollary (Why Published Surrogate Papers Still Report Large Differences): Auditing the primary PDFs and codebases of our 105-paper corpus reveals that zero headline surrogate papers actually run in the true on-policy regime B1 = On-Policy ($r \equiv 1 \implies \text{C1} = \varnothing$): DAPO (2503.14476) and MiniMax-M1 / CISPO (2506.13585) execute $\mu = 16$ gradient updates per rollout batch; DeepSeek-R1 (2501.12948) splits $8{,}192$ rollouts into $\mu = 16$ mini-batches; GMPO (2507.20673) runs $\mu = 8$ updates/round; GSPO (2507.18071) and SAPO (2511.20347) run $\mu = 4$ mini-batches/batch; and verl's default "synchronous" configuration (1024/256) performs $\mu = 4$ updates/batch. Even at $\mu = 1$, computing ratios against rollout-engine log-probabilities $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}$ violates $r \equiv 1$ via Gap 1 (VeXact, 2605.14220 shows bitwise-exact MoE REINFORCE kept improving where the standard vLLM-based run degraded after $\sim 280$ steps, and R3, 2510.11370 shows $\sim 10\%$ of MoE routing decisions differ between training and inference on Qwen3-30B-A3B, affecting $94\%$ of sequences in at least one layer). Proposition 1 therefore proves that every reported accuracy gap between PPO, DAPO, CISPO, GSPO, and SAPO is an indirect measurement of off-policy batch reuse ($\mu \in [4, 16]$ in B1), un-replayed MoE routing drift (B1), or bundled changes to $\hat{A}_i$ ($\div\sigma_G$ in A2) and $c_{i,t}$ ($\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ vs. token-mean in A3).

2.3 From Three PG Slots to Five Code Modules—and Why We Need the Two-Layer Coordinate System ($\text{Target } \mathbf{A} \times \text{Regime } \mathbf{B} \times \text{Approximation } \mathbf{C} \to \text{Dynamics } \mathbf{D}$)

Step 1: Why the three policy-gradient slots expand to five code modules for indexing. The three slots $(\hat{A}_{i,t},\; w_{i,t}m_{i,t},\; c_{i,t})$ of Section 2.2 cover single-turn scalar-reward policy gradients (PPO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO, DAPO, CISPO, GSPO, SAPO). However, five families of post-training papers step outside that single-turn bandit box: (1) non-linear or game-theoretic objectives (pass@$k$ training $\sum_x (1-(1-p_x)^k)$, MaxRL $\sum_x \log p_x$, Nash-MD/SPPO equilibria); (2) alternative supervision signals (process reward models, LLM rubrics, intrinsic self-certainty/entropy, teacher log-probabilities in OPD); (3) non-PG update estimators (closed-form contrastive DPO/IPO/SimPO, rejection-sampled SFT like RAFT/ReST, value-based Q-learning); (4) multi-turn stateful credit assignment (agentic state grouping in GiGPO, environment-token masking in Search-R1); and (5) structured rollout sampling (difficulty curricula, prefix-tree branching in VinePPO/TreeRL). To index which module of a codebase any paper edits, the three slots naturally expand into five operational dimensions: Objective, Signal, Data, Estimator, and Update Control.

Step 2: Why a flat five-axis index cannot predict performance—the Two-Layer Split ($\pi^*$ Target vs. Approximation) and the $\beta = 0$ Simplex Subtlety. A flat five-axis index is useful as a lookup table for where a paper edited code, but treating those five dimensions as parallel peers loses the core causal hierarchy of RL: which dimensions determine where the policy converges (the stationary fixed point or optimal set $\pi^*$), and which dimensions determine how it gets there (bias, variance, speed, and stability)? Moreover, a flat five-axis table misses three critical facts: (i) the base model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ sits on none of the five axes yet fixes the support set $\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ and can flip an algorithm's sign across model families; (ii) the five axes are not orthogonal (advantage normalization is coded inside the Estimator but alters the cross-prompt Objective; Update Control is completely inert unless the Data axis is off-policy); and (iii) exploration dynamics (entropy collapse) form a feedback loop that determines which solutions remain samplable in finite time. Crucially, when $\beta = 0$ and $R(x,y)\in\{0,1\}$ (as in most modern RLVR recipes), $\pi^*$ degenerates from a single interior distribution into the simplex face $\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^+(x))$ of all distributions supported on correct responses ($p_x=1$); on that simplex face, Layer $\mathbf{C}$'s implicit mirror-descent path from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ directly selects which valid solution modes survive, while prompt weighting $F(p_x)$ in A2 governs the finite-step cross-prompt gradient velocity ratio across easy vs. hard prompts.

Returning to the regularized optimal solution $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\big(R(x,y)/\beta\big)$, we elevate the five code modules into a single Two-Layer Coordinate System ($\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}$ conditioned on Cond) read directly from the canonical update equation

$$g(\theta) = \sum_x \underbrace{w(p_x)}_{\text{A2}} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \underbrace{c_{i,t}}_{\text{A3}} \, \underbrace{\psi(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t})}_{\text{C1, C2 (active iff B1 }\ne\text{ on-policy)}} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}) - \underbrace{\beta\,\nabla_\theta \widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}})}_{\text{A4}}$$

and one master diagnostic question: "Did this algorithmic modification change the fixed point $\pi^*$ (Layer A), or did it only alter the approximation path (Layer C) and entropy reachability (Layer D)?"

Quadrant Classification of Post-Training Algorithms: What Each Algorithm Modifies and What Benefit It Brings

Rather than forcing a brittle one-dimensional "leaderboard ranking" across papers that operate under very different models, sequence lengths, and batch-reuse budgets, the clearest way to understand the post-training literature is through three orthogonal $2\times 2$ Quadrant Maps. Each algorithm is a surgical intervention designed for a specific quadrant, trading off one physical bottleneck for a concrete operational benefit:

Quadrant Map I · The $(r_{i,t} - 1,\; \hat{A}_{i,t})$ Trust-Region Plane (Off-Policy Ratio Drift $\times$ Advantage Sign). Every policy-gradient update multiplies the token score $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})$ by an effective scalar force $\psi(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t}) = w(r_{i,t})\,m(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t})\,\hat{A}_{i,t}$. Depending on whether the trajectory succeeded ($\hat{A}_{i,t} > 0$) or failed ($\hat{A}_{i,t} < 0$) and whether the token's probability has risen ($r_{i,t} > 1$) or fallen ($r_{i,t} < 1$) since rollout, every token update lands in one of four distinct physical quadrants:
  • Quadrant I ($\hat{A}_{i,t} > 0,\; r_{i,t} > 1$ — Positive Advantage, Probability Rising: The Exploration & Pivot Expansion Zone):
    What happens here: A token from a successful rollout is being reinforced across multiple micro-steps ($\mu > 1$). Because $r_{i,t} = \pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}$, a multiplicative clip $r_{i,t} \le 1+\varepsilon$ restricts absolute probability growth to $\Delta\pi_{\max} = \varepsilon\,\pi_{\text{old}}$, meaning rare reasoning pivots ($\pi_{\text{old}} = 10^{-3}$) hit the clip wall after $\Delta\pi = 0.0002$ while high-probability tokens ($\pi_{\text{old}} = 0.5$) can shift by $0.10$.
    • PPO / Stock GRPO ($\psi = 0$ for $r > 1.20$): Hard-zeroes the gradient above $1.20$. Benefit: Maximum conservative stability—prevents any single positive batch from over-sharpening the policy or collapsing output diversity in one step.
    • DAPO Clip-Higher ($\psi = 0$ for $r > 1 + \varepsilon_{\text{high}} = 1.28$): Widens only the upper clip boundary from $1.20$ to $1.28$. Benefit: Gives low-probability reasoning and reflection tokens $40\%$ more multiplicative room to grow before cutoff, preventing premature entropy collapse on long-CoT tasks while keeping a hard safety cap.
    • CISPO ($\psi = \operatorname{sg}(\min(r, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}))\hat{A}$): Replaces upper zero-masking with a stop-gradient constant cap $(1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}})\hat{A} > 0$. Benefit: Never discards a positive token across all $\mu = 16$ micro-steps—rare pivot tokens continue receiving a bounded positive update on every step, doubling sample-reuse efficiency.
    • TOPR ($\psi = \hat{A}$ for all $\hat{A} > 0$) & SAPO (smooth bell gate $\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$): TOPR applies an unclipped SFT-like update on all positive trajectories; SAPO replaces the hard step discontinuity at $1+\varepsilon$ with a smooth sigmoid decay. Benefit: TOPR maximizes learning speed when positive rollouts are scarce (low pass@1); SAPO eliminates gradient cliffs at the clip boundary.
  • Quadrant II ($\hat{A}_{i,t} > 0,\; r_{i,t} < 1$ — Positive Advantage, Probability Falling: The Collateral Recovery Zone):
    What happens here: A token from a successful trajectory accidentally had its probability pushed down during earlier micro-steps on other sequences.
    • PPO / GRPO / DAPO ($\psi = r\hat{A}$ unclipped): Because $\min(r\hat{A}, (1-\varepsilon)\hat{A}) = r\hat{A}$ for $\hat{A}>0, r<1-\varepsilon$, the lower clip is inactive. Benefit: Allows the policy to actively pull back up good tokens that suffered collateral downward drift, while naturally attenuating the update by $r$ if the token has drifted far from $\pi_{\text{old}}$.
    • TOPR / Floor-Clamped CISPO ($\psi = \hat{A}$ or $\ge (1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}})\hat{A}$): Maintains a constant positive recovery force even if $r \ll 1$. Benefit: Ensures positive tokens are never lost even under heavy off-policy lag.
  • Quadrant III ($\hat{A}_{i,t} < 0,\; r_{i,t} > 1$ — Negative Advantage, Probability Rising: The Off-Policy Overshoot & Engine-Spike Zone):
    What happens here: A token from a failed trajectory currently has higher probability under $\pi_\theta^{\text{train}}$ than under $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}$—either because collateral updates pushed it up, or because vLLM-to-trainer numerical drift (Gap 1) or MoE expert switching caused $\pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}} \ll \pi_\theta^{\text{train}}$ ($r \gg 1$).
    • PPO / Stock GRPO / DAPO ($\psi = r\hat{A}$ unclipped): Because $\hat{A} < 0$, $\min(r\hat{A}, (1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}) = r\hat{A}$ is completely unclipped for $r > 1$. Benefit: In clean on-policy settings, aggressively corrects bad actions whose probability is mistakenly rising.
    • Dual-Clip PPO ($\psi = \max(r\hat{A}, c\hat{A}),\; c = 3$) & CISPO ($\psi = \operatorname{sg}(\min(r, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}))\hat{A}$): Caps the maximum negative importance weight in Q-III at $c$ or $1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ without zeroing the gradient. Benefit: Still pushes the rising bad token back down, but prevents $r \gg 1$ outlier spikes from exploding the gradient norm.
    • SAPO ($\tau_{\text{neg}} = 4.0 > \tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$), IcePop & R3 Routing Replay (B1 / C1): SAPO damps Q-III $4\times$ faster than Q-I; IcePop masks tokens where the sampler-to-trainer ratio $\rho^{\text{roll}}$ exits $[0.5, 5.0]$; R3 replays the rollout engine's exact MoE expert assignments in B1. Benefit: Eliminates hybrid-engine and MoE routing spikes at their source so Q-III never blows up.
  • Quadrant IV ($\hat{A}_{i,t} < 0,\; r_{i,t} < 1$ — Negative Advantage, Probability Already Suppressed: The Fluency & Prefix-Protection Zone):
    What happens here: A token from a failed rollout has already had its probability reduced ($r < 1$). Because $90\%$ of tokens in a failed CoT are normal syntax or valid intermediate steps, and because the logit score $\nabla_z \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid s_t) = 1 - \pi_\theta(y_t\mid s_t) \to 1$ does not vanish as $\pi_\theta \to 0$, continuing to hammer Q-IV drives logits toward $-\infty$.
    • PPO / GRPO / DAPO ($\psi = 0$ for $r < 1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.80$): Hard-masks the negative gradient once the token's probability has dropped by $20\%$. Benefit: Protects shared reasoning prefixes and model fluency across multiple epochs, preventing negative-logit collapse when failed rollouts dominate (e.g., hard prompts or multi-turn agentic tasks).
    • TOPR ($\psi = \operatorname{sg}(r)\hat{A}$ for $r \in (0, 1)$): Scales the negative gradient linearly by $r = \pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}} \to 0$. Benefit: Smoothly and automatically self-extinguishes as $\pi_\theta \to 0$ without requiring a hard $\varepsilon_{\text{low}}$ cutoff.
    • GSPO / GMPO (C1 = Sequence Geometric Ratio $s_i = (\prod_t r_{i,t})^{1/|y_i|}$): Applies the four-quadrant gate to the geometric-mean ratio of the whole trajectory rather than isolated tokens (computed in FP32 log-space in GMPO). Benefit: Prevents single-token MoE routing noise from triggering chaotic token-by-token masking; keeps the entire sequence update coherent with the sequence-level reward.

Quadrant Map II · The $(p_x,\; \mathcal{H}_t)$ Credit-Allocation Plane (Prompt Pass-Rate $p_x$ $\times$ Token Entropy $\mathcal{H}_t$). Orthogonal to the off-policy ratio $r_{i,t}$, algorithms differ in where they concentrate gradient budget across prompts of varying difficulty $p_x \in [0, 1]$ and tokens of varying branching uncertainty $\mathcal{H}_t = \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t))$:
  • Q1 · Hard Frontier Prompts $\times$ High-Entropy Forking Tokens ($p_x \ll 0.5,\; \mathcal{H}_t$ High — The Capability Expansion Zone):
    What algorithms do here & why: MaxRL (2602.02710, A2 weight $w(p_x) \to 1/p_x$) and Pass@$k$ Policy Gradient (2508.10751) concentrate prompt weight on low-$p_x$ problems, while Token-Masking / Prefix-Tree Credit (SPO, 2505.23564; VinePPO, 2410.01679, C2) localizes updates to branching tokens (`"Wait"`, `"However"`, case splits) rather than uniform sequence credit. Benefit: Concentrates gradient capacity on the reasoning forks that determine whether a hard problem is solved, improving short-window efficiency and $\text{pass@}k$ frontier expansion.
  • Q2 · Hard Prompts $\times$ Low-Entropy Shared Prefix Tokens ($p_x \ll 0.5,\; \mathcal{H}_t \approx 0$ — The Prefix-Corruption Hazard Zone):
    What algorithms do here & why: Many tokens in a reasoning chain are deterministic algebraic steps or shared problem-setup prefixes ($\mathcal{H}_t \approx 0$). On a failed rollout, uniform sequence estimators penalize these correct shared tokens equally with the mistake token—causing DPO Likelihood Displacement and PG variance dilution. Step-DPO, Prefix-Tree Credit (VinePPO/SPO), and Token Masking (C2) zero out or localize gradients away from shared prefixes. Benefit: Prevents failed rollouts from unlearning valid intermediate algebra and syntax.
  • Q3 · Medium-to-Easy Prompts $\times$ Active Variance ($p_x \in [0.2, 0.9]$ — The Pass@1 Consolidation Zone):
    What algorithms do here & why: Dr. GRPO / RLOO (A2=Linear, A3=Const) weights prompts by natural variance $p_x(1-p_x)$ without length distortion, while Original GRPO divides by $\sigma_G = \sqrt{p_x(1-p_x)}$ (A2=Arcsin), boosting both $p_x \to 0$ and $p_x \to 1$. Benefit: Unbiased linear weighting (`Dr. GRPO / RLOO`) steadily consolidates $\text{pass@1}$ reliability without over-amplifying already-solved $p_x \to 1$ prompts or inflating wrong-answer lengths.
  • Q4 · Zero-Variance Dead Prompts ($p_x \in \{0, 1\}$, i.e. $\sum_{i=1}^G R_i \in \{0, G\}$ — The Zero-Bit Zone):
    What algorithms do here & why: When all $G$ rollouts succeed or all $G$ fail, binary outcome RLVR (A1=Verifier) yields $\hat{A}_i \equiv 0$. DAPO Dynamic Sampling and ScaleRL Difficulty Curriculum (D2) filter out $p_x \in \{0, 1\}$ prompts before backward pass; On-Policy Distillation (OPD, A1=Teacher Logprobs) replaces the $0$-bit outcome reward with dense teacher logits $\log \pi_{\text{teacher}}(y_t\mid s_t)$ on every token. Benefit: Dynamic filtering saves wasted backward updates without changing $\pi^*$; OPD turns $p_x = 0$ dead prompts into high-bandwidth learning signals.

2.4 Actionable Engineering Tiering Derived from the Two-Layer Structure

The three practical engineering categories follow directly from the Two-Layer Sufficiency Coordinate System ($\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}$) and the Master Fixed-Point Test (Figure 3):

  • Category I · Fixing Approximation Correctness (Chapter 4): Non-negotiable repairs in A4, B1, C2 that prevent the approximation layer from silently drifting away from the intended fixed point $\pi^*$.
  • Category II · Conditional Off-Policy & Throughput Stabilizers (Chapter 5): Ratio gates and staleness controls in C1, C2 that are empty at B1 = On-Policy ($r \equiv 1 \implies \text{C1} = \varnothing$) but maintain valid convergence when system constraints force B1 into off-policy regimes ($\mu > 1, \tau > 0$, or MoE engine drift).

Any "new loss" that does not alter A1–A4 or prevent D1 entropy collapse can only move convergence speed $B$, never the true capability ceiling $A$.

3. Law V · Statistical Identifiability: Noise Floors, Confounders & Why "Ceiling" Claims Fail

The unified estimator of Chapter 2 tells us what a paper changed algebraically; **Law V (Statistical Identifiability)** tells us whether the reported benchmark delta is mathematically distinguishable from noise. In LLM RL, the evaluation ruler is frequently noisier than the $1\text{–}3$ percentage-point treatment effects papers claim to detect. Before grading any algorithmic mechanism, we pass empirical claims through four quantitative filters.

3.1 Filter 1 · The Three-Source Variance Law & Minimum Detectable Effect ($\text{MDE}_{95\%}$)

1. Combined evaluation-sampling and training-seed variance. Suppose a benchmark has $n$ problems, each evaluated with $k$ rollouts across $S$ independent training seeds. If problem $j$ has conditional pass rate $p_j$ and run-to-run training seed variance is $\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2$, the standard error of the difference $\hat{\Delta} = \hat{R}_A - \hat{R}_B$ between two independently trained and evaluated methods is:

$$\sigma_{\Delta} = \sqrt{2\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p_j(1 - p_j)}{k} + \frac{\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2}{S}\right)} \;\ge\; \sqrt{\frac{2\,\bar{\sigma}_{\text{eval}}^2}{n k} + \frac{2\,\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2}{S}}$$

Even if we ignore training-seed variance ($\sigma_{\text{seed}} = 0$) and consider only evaluation sampling on AIME ($n = 30$ problems, $1\text{ problem} = 3.33\text{ points}$), at the binary variance bound $p_j = 1/2$ the $95\%$ Minimum Detectable Effect ($\text{MDE}_{95\%} \approx 1.96\,\sigma_\Delta$) for comparing two runs is:

Even though real models have many problems with $p_j$ near $0$ or $1$, the order of magnitude is unforgiving: a single-sample AIME comparison cannot distinguish methods differing by less than $\sim 20\text{–}26$ points, and even at avg@32 ($k = 32$) increments under $\sim 4.6$ points sit inside the worst-case $2\sigma_\Delta$ evaluation band.

2. Training-seed variance (rarely measured). Two training runs with different random seeds produce different policies, and no amount of evaluation sampling removes that spread. The few studies that measure it find substantial run-to-run variation: ScaleRL's three repeated runs place the fitted asymptote $A$ within $\pm 0.015$ ($1.5$ percentage points) and treat differences under $\pm 0.02$ as noise (2510.13786); LOOP reports $66.4 \pm 4.8$ on AppWorld across three runs (2502.01600); WPO reports its DPO baseline at $20.6 \pm 0.7$ AlpacaEval LC over five seeds (2406.11827). Most 2025 ablation tables run one seed per row.

3. Evaluation configuration drift (systematic). Hochlehnert et al. re-evaluated published reasoning checkpoints and found that varying sampling temperature shifts AIME scores by up to $\sim 15$ points, top-$p$ by up to $\sim 8$, hardware cluster by up to $\sim 8$, and switching evaluation frameworks moved one model by $4.5$ points (2504.07086). Numbers copied from another paper's table are therefore invalid baselines.

Case Study 3 · Ceiling vs. Efficiency

ScaleRL: What sigmoid compute fits reveal — and where they contradict themselves

ScaleRL (2510.13786) is the only large-scale study ($>400\text{k}$ GB200 GPU-hours across an 8B dense model and Llama-4 Scout MoE on Polaris-53K) that explicitly separates a recipe change's effect on the asymptotic ceiling $A$ from its effect on compute efficiency $B, C_{\text{mid}}$ by fitting validation pass rate $R$ against compute $C$:

$$R(C) = R_0 + \frac{A - R_0}{1 + (C_{\text{mid}} / C)^B}$$

What ScaleRL establishes: (1) Across three repeated runs, $A$ varies by $\pm 0.015$, setting a $\pm 0.02$ noise threshold. (2) Two non-loss knobs clearly lift $A$ beyond noise: batch size ($512 \to 2{,}048$ prompts lifts $A$ from $0.605 \to 0.645$) and generation length ($14\text{k} \to 32\text{k}$ tokens lifts $A$ from $0.610 \to 0.645$ at $4.4\times$ higher $C_{\text{mid}}$). (3) In leave-one-out (LOO) ablations starting from the strong final recipe ($16\text{k}$ GPU-hours per run), removing any single algorithmic component leaves the asymptote $A$ within the $\pm 0.02$ noise band; components only move efficiency $B$ (e.g. replacing CISPO with DAPO lowers $B$ from $2.01$ to $1.77$).

Part III · Category I: Five Laws of Estimator Correctness (Must-Haves)

Non-negotiable mathematical conditions required to keep the autoregressive gradient estimator unbiased across long horizons, hybrid inference-training engines, and MoE routers.

4. Category I · Keeping the Gradient Estimator Honest: Five Correctness Constraints

When the base model, reward verifier, data and compute budget are held fixed, an RL algorithm can only influence the bias and variance of the gradient estimate $g(\theta)$ and the stability of the iterate trajectory. The strongest empirical findings in the 2024–2026 literature — the ones earning A and B across independent groups — are not clever new objectives. They are correctness constraints that stop the implementation from silently computing the wrong gradient.

Constraint 1 (C2 Credit / Baseline) · Unbiased baselines and $\lambda = 1$ for long chains of thought

In critic-free policy gradient, each response $y_i$ in a group of size $G$ receives an advantage $\hat{A}_i = (r_i - b(x))\cdot w(x)$. Because $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)] = 0$, subtracting any baseline $b(x)$ that does not depend on $y_i$ leaves the expected gradient completely unbiased. A simple group mean $\bar{r} = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G r_j$ includes $r_i$ itself, which shrinks the advantage by a deterministic factor $\frac{G-1}{G}$; the leave-one-out mean $\bar{r}_{-i} = \frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{j \neq i} r_j$ (RLOO; 2402.14740) removes it:

$$r_i - \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r_j \;=\; \frac{G - 1}{G}\!\left(r_i - \frac{1}{G - 1}\sum_{j \neq i} r_j\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{A}_i^{\text{RLOO}} \equiv \frac{G}{G - 1}\,\hat{A}_i^{\text{Dr.GRPO}}$$

Thus RLOO and Dr. GRPO (2503.20783) have identical expected gradients up to a scalar $\frac{G}{G-1}$ absorbed by Adam, and differ only by $O(1/G)$ in finite-sample scaling. ReMax (2310.10505) subtracts the reward of the greedy response $r(x, y^{\text{greedy}})$, which is also unbiased, but under binary rewards $r \in \{0, 1\}$ it gives every correct sample zero advantage whenever greedy decoding already solves the prompt.

Why GAE with $\lambda < 1$ fails on long chains of thought. PPO with a learned value network $V_\phi(s_t)$ computes Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). When reward arrives only at the final token $T$, the observed outcome $R(x, y)$ propagates back to token $t$ attenuated by $(\gamma\lambda)^{T-t}$. Under the standard RLHF default $\lambda = 0.95$, the outcome signal decays to $0.95^{100} \approx 0.006$ just $100$ tokens before the end; over a $4{,}000$-to-$8{,}000$ token reasoning trace, early tokens receive zero signal from the true reward and are updated solely on the critic's bootstrap $V_\phi(s_t)$. Even VAPO's length-adaptive schedule $\lambda = 1 - \frac{1}{\alpha \ell}$ with $\alpha = 0.05$ (2504.05118) yields $\lambda \approx 0.9975$ at $\ell = 8{,}000$ — an effective horizon of $\sim 400$ tokens that attenuates the terminal reward at token $1$ by $\lambda^{8000} \approx e^{-20}$. Worse, RLHF pipelines traditionally initialize $V_\phi$ from a reward model trained only to score completed sequences at token $T$, making its predictions on early partial prefixes systematically biased. Setting $\lambda = \gamma = 1$ reduces the advantage to $\hat{A}_{i,t} = R(x, y_i) - V_\phi(s_{i,t})$: the critic becomes a pure state-dependent baseline that reduces variance without introducing bootstrap bias. Four independent studies confirm that $\lambda = 1$ is mandatory for long-CoT PPO (RLOO, 2402.14740; DeepSeek-R1, 2501.12948; Open-Reasoner-Zero, 2503.24290, where $\lambda=0.95$ collapsed response length; VC-PPO, 2503.01491) A.

Case Study 4 · Repairing a Broken Baseline vs. Beating a Tuned Rival

Anatomy of a vanishing 10-point gain: VC-PPO, VAPO, and GenAC

In early 2025, VC-PPO (2503.01491) and VAPO (2504.05118) reported that properly configured value-based PPO beat critic-free GRPO and DAPO by $\sim 10$ AIME points ($48.8$ vs. $38.9$ at $16\text{k}$ context in VC-PPO; $60.4$ vs. $50.0$ in VAPO; and $79$ vs. $73$ on a 150B MoE in Seed1.5-Thinking, 2504.13914). Tracing their ablations reveals two distinct effects:

  1. The internal jump repairs a broken PPO baseline. Both papers start from vanilla PPO with $\lambda = 0.95$ and an RM-initialized critic, which scores $5.6$ (VC-PPO) and $5.0$ (VAPO) on AIME. Pre-training the value network lifts VC-PPO from $5.6 \to 29.4$, and decoupling the critic's $\lambda$ from the actor's lifts it to $41.9$. Similarly, VAPO's three-seed leave-one-out ablation shows that removing value pre-training drops accuracy from $60 \to 11$ and removing decoupled GAE drops it to $33$. That $+36\text{–}49$ point jump is the restoration of an unbiased estimator.
  2. Under a controlled single-variable comparison, the 10-point margin over critic-free methods vanishes. GenAC (2604.10701) held the surrogate loss and hyperparameters fixed on Qwen3-8B-Base across 200 steps and varied only the advantage estimator: GRPO ($50.2$), RLOO ($49.9$) and tuned VC-PPO ($50.4$) landed within half a point; across three seeds, VC-PPO averaged $50.9$ against GRPO's $50.0$, while GRPO cost $0.6\times$ PPO per iteration and VinePPO cost $4.3\times$. Independent re-implementations on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (2504.11343: REINFORCE $53.9$, PPO $52.5$, GRPO $56.3$, filtered REINFORCE $56.4$; and Yue et al., 2504.13837) reach the same conclusion: in single-turn RLVR, a tuned critic buys early sample efficiency at extra memory and compute cost, not a higher ceiling D. Eleven of thirteen frontier reports since 2025 have dropped the critic accordingly (Chapter 9).

Constraint 2 (B1 Off-Policy Regime) · The Two Gaps: sampler–trainer consistency and MoE routing replay

Every hybrid RL framework separates rollout generation ($\pi_{\text{sampler}}$, running in vLLM or SGLang) from gradient computation ($\pi_\theta$, running in Megatron or FSDP). As shown in Figure 6, introducing the trainer's initial forward pass $\pi_{\text{old}}$ splits off-policy drift into two distinct gaps:

  • Gap 1 ($\pi_{\text{sampler}}$ vs. $\pi_{\text{old}}$ at identical weights). Arises from different CUDA kernels, reduction orders, precision (BF16/FP8 inference vs. BF16/FP32 training), top-$p$ truncation, and — most severely — Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) router flips. On Qwen3-30B-A3B, $\sim 10\%$ of expert routing choices differ between inference and training at the exact same weights, so $94\%$ of generated tokens experience a routing flip in at least one layer (R3, 2510.11370).
  • Gap 2 ($\pi_{\text{old}}$ vs. $\pi_\theta$ across updates). Arises from taking $N > 1$ optimizer steps per batch, asynchronous rollout lag, and MoE routing drift across gradient steps.

Because the standard PPO ratio $r_{i,t} = \pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}$ never reads $\pi_{\text{sampler}}$, Gap 1 goes completely uncorrected unless handled explicitly (Yao et al., 2025). Three correctness measures address it at the source:

  1. MoE Routing Replay (R2 / R3) A. Recording the expert indices selected during rollout (R3; 2510.11370) or during the trainer's behavior forward pass (R2; 2512.01374) and replaying them during backpropagation prevents expert-flip instability at $<3\%$ overhead. On Qwen3-30B-A3B, un-replayed GRPO crashed between steps $60$ and $120$ across three setups (and token-level TIS only delayed the crash from step $60$ to $105$), whereas R3 trained stablythroughout (2510.11370). Note that replay slightly alters the objective to optimizing $\pi_\theta$ under frozen routing, which explains why R3 gives no gain strictly on-policy and R2 edges out R3 at low staleness (2512.01374).
  2. Eliminating numerical mismatch at the source B. Running both engines in FP16 (2510.26788) or using batch-invariant deterministic kernels (Thinking Machines, 2025; VeXact, 2605.14220) eliminates Gap 1 completely: in VeXact, bitwise-exact MoE REINFORCE continued improving where vLLM-based training collapsed around step $280$. A cheaper partial fix is keeping the LM output head in FP32 (MiniMax-M1, 2506.13585), which helped Llama-4 Scout MoE in ScaleRL though it did not prevent collapse alone in two other studies D.
  3. Monitoring sampler–trainer KL for hardware/kernel bugs. In one diagnostic study, an attention optimization in vLLM on A100 GPUs inflated sampler–trainer KL by two orders of magnitude, and a collapsing run recovered simply by resuming on a different GPU type or disabling the kernel flag (Liu & Li, 2025).

Constraint 3: An unbiased KL estimator is not an unbiased KL loss gradient. Write $\delta_t = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid\cdot)/\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)$ for a sampled token. Three per-token estimators of $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ are ubiquitous: $k_1 = -\log\delta_t$, $k_2 = \frac{1}{2}(\log\delta_t)^2$, and $k_3 = \delta_t - 1 - \log\delta_t$. $$\Delta \mathcal{H}(s) \;\approx\; -\eta\,\operatorname{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\!\Big(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s),\; \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\,\hat{A}(s, a)\Big)$$

Entropy drops fastest when already-likely tokens ($-\log \pi_\theta$ small) receive positive advantages. Two practices strongly amplify this covariance: (1) taking multiple SGD steps per rollout batch ($N > 1$), which repeatedly pushes up the same positive-advantage tokens, causing rapid entropy collapse around step $100$ in Skywork-OR1 (2505.22312), AceReason (2505.16400), and Magistral (2506.10910, which degraded beyond $2$ minibatches per batch) A; and (2) sampling at low temperature ($T = 0.6$ collapsed entropy within $\sim 100$ steps in Skywork-OR1) B. Crucially, adding a fixed-coefficient entropy bonus $-\alpha \mathcal{H}$ does not solve this: small coefficients do nothing while slightly larger ones cause entropy to explode into gibberish across both the Entropy Mechanism study and Magistral A.

5. Category II · Law III (Softmax Logit-Jacobian Asymmetry), Trust Regions & Off-Policy Corrections

Coordinate C1 (4-quadrant ratio clipping/gating) and Coordinate B1 (off-policy batch reuse $\mu$, async lag $\tau$, and sampler–trainer mismatch) account for the majority of named RL algorithms proposed in 2025–2026: PPO-clip, dual-clip, clip-higher (DAPO), CISPO, GSPO, GMPO, SAPO, TOPR, TIS, IcePop, M2PO, and more. By Theorem 2 (the $r \equiv 1$ identity), every one of these methods vanishes in the strictly on-policy regime (B1 = On-Policy $\implies$ C1 = ∅). They exist because hardware physics forces high-throughput RL runs off-policy—by reusing each rollout batch for $\mu = 4\text{–}16$ gradient steps, streaming asynchronous rollouts $\tau = 2\text{–}8$ policy versions stale, or running quantized inference on MoE models. To denoise all of these surrogates at once, we derive their behavior from a single equation: the Softmax Logit Jacobian.

5.1 Law III · The Softmax Logit-Jacobian Asymmetry & the Four $(r, \hat{A})$ Quadrants

Consider a single token step $t$ with vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, pre-softmax logits $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$, and policy $\pi_\theta(v \mid s_t) = \operatorname{softmax}(z_t)_v$. Under any surrogate with effective weight $W_t = w(r_t)\,m(r_t, \hat{A}_t)$, a gradient ascent step $\Delta z_t = \eta \frac{\partial \hat{\mathcal{J}}}{\partial z_t}$ updates the sampled token $y_t$ and every unsampled token $v \ne y_t$ by:

$$\Delta z_{t, y_t} = +\eta\,\hat{A}_t\,W_t\,\big(1 - \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)\big), \qquad \Delta z_{t, v} = -\eta\,\hat{A}_t\,W_t\,\pi_\theta(v \mid s_t) \quad (\forall\, v \ne y_t)$$

Theorem 3 (Law III · Three Fundamental Asymmetries of Autoregressive Policy Gradients). The Softmax logit update above is fundamentally asymmetric between positive advantage ($\hat{A}_t > 0$) and negative advantage ($\hat{A}_t < 0$):

  1. Self-extinguishing positive push vs. non-extinguishing negative push: When $\hat{A}_t > 0$, as $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t) \to 1$, the Jacobian factor $(1 - \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)) \to 0$—the gradient automatically brakes as the token is mastered ($\hat{A}_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t) \le 0$ is bounded above). When $\hat{A}_t < 0$, pushing $y_t$ down drives $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t) \to 0$, where $(1 - \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)) \to 1$ never self-extinguishes ($-|\hat{A}_t|\log\pi_\theta(y_t) \to +\infty$), causing unbounded logit-norm drift $z_{t, y_t} \to -\infty$.
  2. Negative gradients directly cause entropy collapse (rich-get-richer mode amplification): Look at the unsampled tokens $v \ne y_t$ when $\hat{A}_t < 0$: $$\Delta z_{t, v} = +\eta\,|\hat{A}_t|\,W_t\,\pi_\theta(v \mid s_t) \quad (\forall\, v \ne y_t)$$ Pushing a bad token $y_t$ down does not redistribute probability mass uniformly; it increases the logits of unsampled tokens in exact proportion to their current probability $\pi_\theta(v \mid s_t)$, concentrating mass exponentially onto the current argmax mode $\arg\max_v \pi_\theta(v \mid s_t)$.
  3. Rare-token clipping starvation in Quadrant I ($\hat{A}_t > 0,\; r_t > 1 + \varepsilon_{\text{high}}$): Because PPO's upper clip bounds the multiplicative ratio $\pi_\theta(y_t) / \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t) \le 1 + \varepsilon_{\text{high}}$, the maximum probability gain across a batch of $\mu$ micro-steps is $\Delta \pi_{\max}(y_t) = \varepsilon_{\text{high}}\,\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t)$. A rare reasoning pivot token (e.g., `"Wait"`, `"However"`) with $\pi_{\text{old}} = 10^{-3}$ hits the clip wall after gaining just $2\times 10^{-4}$, whereas a dominant mode token with $\pi_{\text{old}} = 0.5$ can gain $0.10$.

The relative-clip entropy bias — and what Clip-higher and CISPO actually measure

Why symmetric ratio clipping lowers entropy. Because PPO's upper bound $r_{i,t} \le 1 + \varepsilon$ is multiplicative, a token with old probability $p_{\text{old}}$ can increase in a single batch by at most $\Delta p = \varepsilon\, p_{\text{old}}$. For a rare reasoning token ($p_{\text{old}} = 10^{-4}$), that ceiling is tiny; for a dominant token ($p_{\text{old}} = 0.5$), it is huge. Positive updates on low-probability tokens are therefore cut off early while high-probability tokens absorb the probability mass, driving entropy down even under random rewards (2509.26114).

  • Clip-higher ($\varepsilon_{\text{high}} > \varepsilon_{\text{low}}$, DAPO) B mechanism · D score gain. Raising $\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ from $0.20$ to $0.28$ relaxes the ceiling on positive-advantage tokens. Its entropy-preserving mechanism is reliable, but its score impact depends entirely on how often tokens hit the upper clip edge: on Qwen3-4B/8B base models, the upper clip fires on only $\sim 0.3\%$ of tokens and raising $\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ does nothing or hurts, whereas on instruction-tuned models (which start sharper and lose entropy faster) raising $\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ slows entropy collapse and improves scores (Tricks or Traps, 2508.08221). Across other studies, the Entropy Mechanism paper reports $+0.2$ (7B) and $+1.4$ (32B) points with late instability (2505.22617), ScaleRL finds $A$ rises and then falls as $\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ increases (2510.13786), and MiniMax-M1 found clip-higher insufficient at $N = 16$ updates per batch. Thus "which model clip-higher helps" is a proxy for how far off-policy the batch drifts.
  • CISPO (MiniMax-M1, 2506.13585) B large-batch efficiency · D general. At $N = 16$ updates per rollout batch, MiniMax-M1 observed that rare reflection tokens (However, Wait) crossed $1 + \varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ on the first minibatch and were zero-masked for the remaining fifteen. CISPO replaces the mask with a stop-gradient clipped weight $\operatorname{sg}(\min(r_{i,t}, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}))$ and disables the lower clip bound. In ScaleRL's large-batch LOO runs, CISPO tied DAPO's asymptote $A$ while raising efficiency $B$ from $1.77$ to $2.01$. However, reopening the $\hat{A} < 0,\, r \ll 1$ corner (Figure 7b, right) has a severe failure mode: in ARLArena's agentic tasks, both CISPO and SAPO collapsed around step $130$ ($34.0$ vs. GRPO's $46.2$), and adding sequence-level masking of negative low-ratio trajectories restored ALFWorld accuracy from $54.4 \to 78.9$ (2602.21534; see also CE-GPPO, 2509.20712). Note also an implementation trap: verl's default cispo config clips weights symmetrically to $[0.8, 1.2]$ unless the lower bound is manually widened (Chapter 9).
  • The 2025–2026 tail (GMPO, SAPO, DCPO, CE-GPPO, ASPO, DPPO, DGPO) C. All re-parameterize the same complaint — that a constant ratio band $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$ is too tight for small $p_{\text{old}}$ and too loose for large $p_{\text{old}}$. Each reports $1\text{–}3$ point single-seed gains in its originating paper, usually on Qwen2.5-Math or 1.5B models under $1{,}000$ steps.

Token vs. sequence granularity, importance sampling, and GSPO

When correcting for off-policy drift (Gap 2) or sampler–trainer mismatch (Gap 1), should the ratio act per token or across the whole sequence? Both extremes face a fundamental mathematical obstacle at reasoning lengths ($T = 8\text{k–}32\text{k}$):

  1. Token-level Truncated IS (TIS) leaves a prefix-distribution bias D. Multiplying token $t$'s gradient by $\min(\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t\mid y_{<t})/\pi_{\text{sampler}}(y_t\mid y_{<t}),\, C)$ bounds per-token variance by $C^2$. However, it corrects only the conditional distribution of action $y_t$ given prefix $y_{<t}$, leaving uncorrected the fact that the prefix $y_{<t}$ itself was sampled from $\pi_{\text{sampler}}$. The residual bias grows quadratically with horizon $T$ times the maximum per-token divergence (Liu & Li, 2025), which explains why token-level TIS alone only delayed MoE collapse from step $60$ to $105$ in R3 (2510.11370) and gave zero gain on short BF16 dense runs (Yao et al., 2025).
  2. Exact sequence-level IS suffers exponential variance blowup. The unbiased sequence importance weight is $\rho(y) = \prod_{t=1}^T \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t})}{\mu(y_t\mid y_{<t})}$. If per-token log-ratios have variance $\sigma^2 \approx 2\,\mathrm{KL}_{\text{token}}$, then $\log \rho(y)$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{T}$. Using R3's measured MoE per-token mismatch $\mathrm{KL}_{\text{token}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$ gives $\sigma \approx 0.055$. At $T = 16{,}000$ tokens:
    $$\operatorname{Std}\!\big(\log \rho(y)\big) = \sigma\sqrt{T} \;\approx\; 0.055 \times \sqrt{16{,}000} \;\approx\; 7.0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{\pm 7.0} \approx [9\times 10^{-4},\; 1{,}100]$$
    One standard deviation spans a factor of $1{,}100\times$ in either direction! Any capped sequence weight $\min(\rho(y), C)$ therefore degenerates into a binary rejection filter.
  3. Length-normalized geometric filters (GSPO, Geometric RS, DeepSeek-V3.2) are trust-region tests in disguise. To keep sequence-level statistics from scaling with $T$, GSPO (2507.18071) replaces the product with the geometric mean $s_i = \exp\!\big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log r_{i,t}\big)$ and clips $s_i$ inside a narrow band $[1 - 3\cdot 10^{-4},\, 1 + 4\cdot 10^{-4}]$. Exactly the same length-normalized log-ratio statistic appears in geometric rejection sampling (Liu & Li, 2025) and DeepSeek-V3.2's off-policy sequence masking (2512.02556). Note two caveats on GSPO: differentiating $s_i$ re-introduces the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ length bias (Chapter 6), and while GSPO stabilized Qwen3-30B-A3B in its own paper (clipping $15\%$ of tokens vs. GRPO's $0.13\%$), Qwen's later controlled study on the exact same model needed neither GSPO nor sequence clipping once train–inference IS and routing replay (R2/R3) were enabled (2512.01374). Elsewhere, GSPO ranked first in ARLArena, diverged repeatedly in ScaleRL, and tied the untrained base model on Qwen1.5-MoE across three seeds ($28.90 \pm 1.25$ vs. $28.93$; CIS, 2609.32444) B un-replayed MoE · D general.

Truncating vs. masking, and protecting the negative quadrant. In the one multi-seed comparison of mismatch fixes across three MoE models (CIS, 2609.32444), no correction, TIS, sequence IS, IcePop (2510.18855), GSPO, FP16 and CIS all finished within $\sim 1.6$ average points on short GSM8K runs, but one manipulation was catastrophic: lifting small importance weights upward cut Qwen1.5-MoE accuracy from $34.8$ to $6.0$ (well below the $28.9$ base model). Across the literature, independent designs converge on protecting the $\hat{A} < 0,\, r \ll 1$ corner: TOPR truncates IS weights only on negative samples (2503.14286); DeepSeek-V3.2 masks only negative-advantage sequences that fail its KL drift threshold (2512.02556); and M2PO constrains second moments only on tokens clipping would act on (2510.01161).

How much off-policy staleness is tolerable? The $S \cdot \eta$ law

Asynchronous rollout frameworks (AReaL, PipelineRL, Magistral, OLMo 3) trade staleness $S$ (in policy versions) for hardware utilization. Controlled measurements establish three regimes:

  • Without off-policy correction: $S \le 1\text{–}2$ versions. Magistral degraded beyond $2$ minibatches per rollout (2506.10910); AReaL's 1.5B ablation stayed within $2$ AIME points of on-policy ($42.0$) up to staleness $2$ before dropping (2505.24298).
  • With decoupled PPO or truncated IS: $S \approx 4\text{–}8$ versions B. AReaL's decoupled objective (which anchors the clip around a proximal policy while weighting by behavior-to-proximal IS) matches on-policy accuracy up to $S = 8$ at $2.77\times$ wall-clock speed (2505.24298). ScaleRL found PipelineRL in-flight weight updates at lag $4$ and $8$ equally effective, cutting time-to-reward by $\sim 2\times$ (2510.13786). (M2PO claims parity at $S = 256$ in a single best-checkpoint run C.)
  • Staleness is a product budget $S \cdot \eta \approx \text{const}$. A 2026 study across Llama-3.2-1B/3B and Qwen3-8B shows that the maximum stable learning rate $\eta_{\max}$ scales inversely with staleness $S$, so the product $S \cdot \eta$ is approximately constant along the stability boundary (2607.01083). Because first-order parameter drift over $S$ steps scales as $O(S\eta)$, quoting a "safe staleness $S$" without the learning rate $\eta$ is meaningless: a run at $\eta = 10^{-6}$ tolerates roughly three times the version lag of a run at $3\times 10^{-6}$.

6. Category III · Implicit Objectives: Prompt Normalization $F(p)$ & Length Aggregation

Unlike the off-policy ratio gates of Chapter 5 (which sit in Approximation Layer C1 and vanish when $r \equiv 1$), the normalizers in the canonical estimator — the per-prompt advantage scale $s(x)$ (Target Coordinate A2) and the per-token length aggregation weight $c_{i,t}$ (Target Coordinate A3) — remain active even at $r \equiv 1$. The literature has debated them as estimator variance-reduction tricks and produced contradictory ablations. The contradictions resolve once we write out their expectations: neither knob merely improves gradient estimation; both are disguised Target-Layer modifications that alter the stationary fixed point $\pi^*$.

Coordinate A2 · Advantage normalization chooses a pass-rate transform $F(p)$

Consider a binary outcome reward $r \in \{0, 1\}$, and let $p_x = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}[r(x, y)]$ be the current pass rate on prompt $x$. Because $\mathbb{E}[(r - p_x)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta] = \nabla_\theta p_x$, dividing a prompt's centered rewards by a scale $s(p_x)$ produces the expected gradient (2607.00152):

$$\mathbb{E}\big[g(\theta)\big] \;=\; \sum_{x} \frac{\nabla_\theta p_x}{s(p_x)} \;=\; \nabla_\theta \sum_{x} F(p_x), \qquad \text{where } F'(p) = \frac{1}{s(p)}$$

Every advantage scaling rule is therefore exact gradient ascent on a transformed objective $\sum_x F(p_x)$ in the large-group limit:

Resolving the GRPO vs. Dr. GRPO debate via $F''(p)$. Differentiating GRPO's implicit objective $F(p) = 2\arcsin\sqrt{p}$ twice reveals why dividing by the group standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$ is really two opposing interventions bundled together:

$$F'(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1 - p)}}, \qquad F''(p) = -\frac{1 - 2p}{2\big(p(1 - p)\big)^{3/2}}$$
  • For hard prompts ($p < 1/2$), $F''(p) < 0$ ($F$ is concave). GRPO up-weights rare successes on hard prompts — in a group of $G = 8$ with a single correct sample ($p = 1/8$), GRPO gives the winning response an advantage of $+2.50\text{ to }+2.65$ (depending on $G$ vs. $G-1$ in the std denominator) against $+0.875$ under Dr. GRPO. Up-weighting low-$p$ prompts is well-motivated: both MaxRL ($\log p$) and pass@$k$ training are strictly concave on $(0, 1)$ for the exact same reason, preventing the policy from abandoning hard problems (Chapter 1).
  • For easy prompts ($p > 1/2$), $F''(p) > 0$ ($F$ is convex). Because $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$ is symmetric around $p = 1/2$, GRPO gives the exact same $\sim 3\times$ amplification to a prompt the model already solves $7/8$ of the time! No optimization principle argues for spending maximum gradient step size on nearly mastered prompts, and on easy-heavy datasets this convex tail amplifies noise and destabilizes updates.
  • Batch-level standard deviation is a global scalar. Dividing by the standard deviation across the entire batch (REINFORCE++, ScaleRL, AReaL, Magistral) rescales all prompts equally, leaving $F(p) = p$ unchanged; under scale-invariant Adam, its only effect is stabilizing global gradient norms near the optimizer's $\varepsilon$ and clipping threshold.

Controlled evidence on Coordinate A2 matches the decomposition exactly:

  • On the pass@1 asymptote, std division is neutral A. ScaleRL found prompt-level, batch-level and no std normalization "similar" (2510.13786); Magistral observed no significant effect on accuracy or length growth (2506.10910); and the minimalist comparison found little gain beyond discarding all-wrong groups (2504.11343).
  • On efficiency and stability, the sign tracks dataset difficulty D. Tricks or Traps (2508.08221) found that removing GRPO's group std helps on easy datasets (where $p > 1/2$ convexity hurts) and is neutral on hard datasets, recommending group-mean centering with batch-level std. In agentic RL, LOOP found group std division cost several points on AppWorld (2502.01600). Meanwhile, asymmetric hard-prompt weighting wins on pass@$k$ (MaxRL improved pass@$k$ in $7$ of $8$ settings, 2602.02710; Pass@$k$ training raised Llama-3.1-8B pass@8 on AIME24 from $32.1 \to 40.0$, 2508.10751) C.

Why $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ creates a length bias (exact covariance decomposition) B. Because $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$, the true policy gradient of sequence reward $\mathbb{E}[R(x,y)]$ sums over tokens with weight $1$, not $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ (Qwen, 2512.01374). Dividing each response by its own length $|y_i|$ introduces a covariance bias between inverse length and sequence score:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[\frac{R(x,y) - p_x}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\right]\nabla_\theta p_x + \operatorname{Cov}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|},\; \big(R(x,y) - p_x\big)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\right)$$

For positive-advantage responses ($R - p_x > 0$), the factor $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ rewards short correct answers more per token (brevity bias); for negative-advantage responses ($R - p_x < 0$), it penalizes long wrong answers less per token ($-\frac{|R - p_x|}{|y_i|}$), creating a direct gradient incentive to stretch failed trajectories longer to dilute the negative penalty (Dr. GRPO, 2503.20783). Note also a silent implementation divergence: DAPO normalizes token-mean by the tokens in each prompt's group, whereas verl's token-mean normalizes by all valid tokens across the global PPO minibatch (Chapter 9). Empirically, while removing $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ stops wrong-answer length inflation, whether token-mean beats seq-mean-token-mean on benchmark scores flips between base models and instruction-tuned models (2508.08221) and stays within noise in ScaleRL's LOO runs D.

7. Levers Outside the Loss: Batch Size, Generation Budget, Filtering, and Verifiers

Steps 1 and 2 of the training loop — which prompts are sampled, how long rollouts may run, and how responses are scored — sit outside the policy-gradient loss function. Yet in controlled compute-scaling studies, these external inputs are the only within-RL knobs that reliably move the pass@1 asymptote $A$, while verifier precision determines whether the target distribution $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R/\beta)$ aligns with truth or collapses into reward hacking.

Boundary Condition Cond · What actually raises the pass@1 ceiling: batch size and generation length

In ScaleRL's sigmoid scaling fits (Case Study 3; 2510.13786), two sampling parameters move the asymptote $A$ well beyond the $\pm 0.02$ repeat-run noise floor B:

  • Rollout batch size. Increasing the batch from $512 \to 768 \to 2{,}048$ prompts raises the fitted asymptote from $A \approx 0.605 \to 0.610 \to 0.645$. Smaller batches appear faster at small compute budgets ($C < C_{\text{mid}}$) and are overtaken later, even though per-step entropy trajectories look nearly identical.
  • Generation length budget. Extending maximum rollout length from $14\text{k}$ to $32\text{k}$ tokens lifts $A$ from $0.610$ to $0.645$, at the cost of a $4.4\times$ increase in the compute needed to reach the midpoint ($C_{\text{mid}}: 2{,}542 \to 11{,}272$). Longer budgets expand the set of multi-step verification and backtracking trajectories the policy is allowed to complete.
  • Group size $G$ at fixed total batch is second-order. Holding total batch tokens fixed while varying $G \in \{8, 16, 24, 32\}$ yields $A \in \{0.585, 0.610, 0.590, 0.595\}$ — a $0.025$ spread at the edge of noise with no monotone trend.

7.2 Information-Theoretic Filtering ($H_{\text{Ber}}(p_x)$) & Truncation Dynamics

Zero-variance filtering as Shannon information maximization B efficiency/stability · D ceiling. For a prompt $x$ with binary pass rate $p_x \in [0, 1]$, each rollout $Y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ carries Shannon mutual information with the verifier outcome $R \in \{0, 1\}$ equal to the binary entropy:

$$I(R;\, Y \mid x) = H_{\text{Ber}}(p_x) = -p_x \log_2 p_x - (1 - p_x)\log_2(1 - p_x) \in [0, 1]\text{ bit}, \qquad \Pr(\text{all } G \text{ equal} \mid p_x) = p_x^G + (1 - p_x)^G$$

When $p_x \in \{0, 1\}$ (or whenever a finite sample of $G$ rollouts yields $r_1 = \dots = r_G$, which occurs with probability $p_x^G + (1-p_x)^G$), the empirical group carries zero bits of contrast information and every advantage $\hat{A}_i$ is identically zero. That group contributes zero numerator gradient, yet under token-mean aggregation its tokens still enter the denominator $\sum_j |y_j|$, randomly attenuating the effective learning rate across batches. Dynamic sampling (DAPO, 2503.14476) and zero-variance filtering discard such $0$-bit groups and refill the batch until $0 < \sum_{i=1}^G r_i < G$, while difficulty curricula drop mastered prompts with $p_x \to 1$ (AceReason, 2505.16400). Filtering maximizes informative gradient steps per update, though refilling carries a rollout rejection cost ($41\%$ of samples survive at $G = 16$ vs. $62\%$ at $G = 512$ in BroRL, 2510.01180), leaves the asymptote $A$ unchanged in ScaleRL's leave-one-out runs, and proved unnecessary when using a constant normalizer $M$ in JustRL (2512.16649) and on Countdown (2512.07611).

Overlong truncation handling and length curricula D. When a rollout hits the maximum token cap without finishing, DAPO masks it out of the loss and adds a soft length penalty ($+6$ and $+3$ in Table 1). Subsequent independent studies split sharply based on the length budget:

  • At a tight budget ($8\text{k}$ tokens), masking truncated responses helps clearly by removing noisy negative gradients on valid reasoning chains that merely ran out of room (Tricks or Traps, 2508.08221).
  • At a generous budget ($20\text{k–}32\text{k}$ tokens), the benefit vanishes or reverses: OLMo 3 found no consistent gain (2512.13961), JustRL found DAPO's soft overlong penalty lowered AIME24 by $\sim 5$ points and collapsed entropy (2512.16649), and Skywork-OR1 warns that masking truncated trajectories at $32\text{k}$ removes the only penalty on runaway rambling — a form of length reward hacking (2505.22312). One diagnostic holds across setups: a truncation rate climbing past $10\text{–}15\%$ reliably precedes training instability (ScaleRL) B.
  • Length curricula (training short then long) split the same way: used in Skywork-OR1 and AceReason, but found irreversibly worse than a single $64\text{k}$ stage in GLM-4.5 (2508.06471) and harmful on Qwen3-4B in Polaris.

Coordinates A1 & A4 · Verifier asymmetry, label-free sharpening, and when anchor KL is load-bearing

Why false positives and false negatives are fundamentally asymmetric B. Suppose a binary verifier has false-negative rate $\alpha = \Pr(\hat{r}=0 \mid r=1)$ and false-positive rate $\beta = \Pr(\hat{r}=1 \mid r=0)$. If those error rates are uniform across responses, the observed pass rate on prompt $x$ is $\tilde{p}_x = (1 - \alpha - \beta)p_x + \beta$, so the expected gradient is simply rescaled:

$$\nabla_\theta \tilde{p}_x \;=\; (1 - \alpha - \beta)\,\nabla_\theta p_x$$

Uniform label noise slows learning without biasing the optimum. In reality, verifier errors are response-dependent, and the two error types behave asymmetrically under $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R/\beta)$:

  1. False negatives waste signal (an efficiency loss). Rule-based math verifiers achieve $>99\%$ precision but average only $86\%$ recall — and one popular parser recalled just $47\%$ of valid answers on WebInstruct (2505.22203). TinyV found that $38.5\%$ of responses marked wrong in a verified math dataset were actually correct; adding a small neural verifier only as a second pass on rejected answers recovered $+2.6\text{–}2.8$ points (2505.14625).
  2. False positives create an exploitable mode (a ceiling catastrophe). Any response $y_{\text{hack}}$ that fools the verifier into returning $\hat{r} = 1$ (like candidate y7 in Figure 1) becomes a global maximizer of the objective. In 2505.22203, a $1.5\text{B}$ generative verifier used directly as the reward was quickly hacked into accepting a single "{" character or gibberish, collapsing training (whereas a discriminative verifier resisted exploitation). Frontier recipes reflect this law: Kimi k1.5 removes multiple-choice, true/false, proof, and guessable prompts from its RL set (2501.12599), and DeepSeek-R1 avoids neural reward models during reasoning RL (2501.12948).

Label-free rewards obey the sharpening bound B limits · D general. Methods that train without ground-truth labels — entropy minimization (2505.15134), test-time majority-vote rewards (TTRL, 2504.16084), and self-certainty (2505.19590) — confirm Pattern 3: TTRL lifted Mistral-Nemo by $9\text{–}10$ points on AMC and MATH-500 (where its mode is often right) while driving AIME 2024 from $0.8 \to 0$ (where its mode is wrong), and offline self-certainty was hacked by appending already-solved problems to the output.

When to drop vs. keep the KL anchor to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (Coordinate A4) B. Layer 2 of Chapter 1 explains why the role of $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ splits cleanly by verifier type:

  • With a high-precision rule-based verifier, drop the anchor KL to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Because the verifier cannot easily be fooled (`y7` is absent), holding the policy near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ only caps how far $\pi^*$ can sharpen: in Skywork-OR1, setting $\beta = 10^{-3}$ in stage 2 caused AIME24 to plateau, and every released checkpoint uses $\beta = 0$ (2505.22312; see also 2505.22617). DAPO, Magistral, GLM-4.5, MiniMax-M1, ScaleRL, OLMo 3 and JustRL all set $\beta = 0$. Systems that keep a KL term use a proximal reference instead: DeepSeek-R1 resets $\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$ every $400$ steps at $\beta = 0.001$ (2501.12948v2), ProRL resets on validation stalls (2505.24864), and Kimi k1.5 regularizes toward the previous iterate $\pi_k$ (2501.12599) C.
  • With a learned reward model or rubric judge, the anchor KL is mandatory. When $R$ is a neural proxy or LLM judge (Rubrics as Rewards, 2507.17746), true quality follows Gao et al.'s hump-shaped over-optimization law $R_{\text{gold}}(d) \approx d(\alpha - \beta \log d)$ in $d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})}$ (2210.10760; InstructGPT, 2203.02155). In a careful PPO replication, 1B runs that drifted to $\mathrm{KL} \in [50, 85]$ maximized the proxy RM while dropping below a $20\%$ human win rate (2403.17031). 8. Denoising the Wider Spectrum: DPO, Process Rewards, Agentic RL & On-Policy Distillation

Earlier chapters established that hundreds of single-turn RLVR papers reduce to variations on one estimator. What about the thousands of papers in the four neighbouring post-training families—Preference Optimization (DPO), Process Reward Models (PRMs), Multi-Turn Agentic RL, and On-Policy Distillation (OPD)? Applying Laws I–IV denoises all four families with the exact same mathematical precision.

8.1 Family 1 · Preference Optimization (Denoising 1,000+ DPO Variants): Loss Shapes Tie, On-Policy Data Wins

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO, 2305.18290) inverts Layer 2's closed-form Gibbs optimality equation $\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R(x,y)/\beta)$ to write the implicit reward as $R_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ and substitutes it into a pairwise preference loss over winning and losing completions $(y_w, y_l)$. More generally, nearly every offline preference algorithm in the literature minimizes a convex contrast function $\psi$ of the log-ratio margin:

$$\mathcal{L}_\psi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mu_{\text{pair}}}\!\Big[\psi\!\big(\beta\,\rho_\theta(x, y_w) - \beta\,\rho_\theta(x, y_l)\big)\Big], \qquad \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\psi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\text{pair}}}\!\Big[\beta\,\psi'(\Delta_\theta)\Big(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big]$$

Look at the gradient on the right: every preference loss in the DPO family is simply a scalar margin-weighting function $-\beta\psi'(\Delta_\theta)$ multiplying the exact same positive-minus-negative score vector $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$. Why, then, does offline preference optimization hit an early ceiling while online RL keeps improving? Apply Law III's Softmax Jacobian to the negative term $-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)$ when $(y_w, y_l) \sim \mu_{\text{off}}$ is sampled from an offline dataset rather than $\pi_\theta$: pushing $\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)$ down redistributes probability mass onto $\pi_\theta$'s current modal tokens along the prefix of $y_l$ via $\Delta z_{t, v} \propto +\pi_\theta(v \mid s_t)$—not onto $y_w$! When $\mu_{\text{off}} \ne \pi_\theta$, this leaks probability mass into unconstrained out-of-distribution states and can even decrease $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$ (likelihood displacement, 2410.08847). Controlled studies confirm both predictions of this equation:

  • Convex link functions tie once $\beta$ and learning rate are tuned A. GPO unified six convex preference losses (including DPO, IPO and SLiC) and showed they reach similar peak performance at matched KL once $\beta$ is swept (2402.05749). A 2025 grid study across $\beta$ and learning rates on Llama-3.1-8B found DPO, IPO, SimPO and ORPO finishing within $\sim 1.5$ AlpacaEval LC points of each other (2502.01237).
  • Data on-policyness dominates the choice of loss (Pattern 6) A. With offline pairs generated by other models, the DPO objective leaves out-of-sample directions unconstrained, allowing probability mass to leak away from both $y_w$ and $y_l$ (likelihood displacement, 2410.08847). Sampling pairs from the current policy closes that gap: in WPO's ablation (2406.11827, Table 1), replacing off-policy pairs with samples from the SFT model adds $+16\text{–}17$ AlpacaEval LC points, whereas switching the loss from DPO to WPO on fixed data adds $+1.6\text{–}5.6$. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, semi-online and online DPO match GRPO on MATH-500 ($58.9$ and $58.7$ vs. $58.1$) and beat it on AlpacaEval LC, while offline DPO trails by $4\text{–}5$ points on math and $>20$ on chat (2506.21495; see also Ivison et al., 2406.09279; Xu et al., 2404.10719; Llama 3, 2407.21783).
  • Length normalization and NLL anchors help offline; implicit over-optimization persists B. Tulu 3 found length-normalized DPO superior to vanilla DPO and SimPO (2411.15124), and adding an NLL anchor on $y_w$ mitigates likelihood displacement on offline pairs (though it adds nothing on-policy; 2506.21495). Direct preference losses also follow the exact same hump-shaped over-optimization curve in $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$ as RLHF (Rafailov et al., 2406.02900). In modern pipelines, Tulu 3 and OLMo 3 (2512.13961) place length-normalized DPO before RLVR, whereas Llama 4 applies only a light DPO pass after online RL so offline preferences do not restrict exploration (Meta, 2025).

Family 2 · Process rewards and dense credit assignment

Outcome-reward RL assigns a single scalar $\hat{A}_i$ to every token in a multi-thousand-token trace. Process supervision attempts to sharpen credit assignment by scoring intermediate steps. Here the mathematical divide between unbiased Monte Carlo estimation and learned proxy rewards is razor-sharp:

Potential-based shaping vs. proxy accumulation (Pattern 5). Suppose per-step signals $\phi_t$ are added to the outcome reward $R(x, y)$. By Ng, Harada & Russell (1999), the optimal policy $\pi^*$ is preserved for every MDP if and only if $\phi_t$ is a telescoping potential difference:

$$R'(x, y) = R(x, y) + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \phi_t \quad\text{is unhackable if}\quad \phi_t = \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t) \;\Longrightarrow\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \phi_t = \Phi(s_{T+1}) - \Phi(s_1)$$

Whenever $\phi_t$ is a free learned score from a Process Reward Model (PRM) summed across steps without telescoping, the policy can inflate $\sum_{t=1}^T \phi_t$ without bound simply by emitting more steps that flatter the scorer.

  • Learned PRMs summed into the return are hacked in every independent study A. In Gao et al. (2410.15115), adding a learned PRM to outcome reward caused Qwen2-1.5B MATH accuracy to crash from its $24.90$ starting point to $11.16$ within one epoch (while outcome reward alone reached $30.58$ at epoch 5) as the model learned to emit dozens of trivial single-word or emoji steps. PURE (2504.15275) found summed PRMs collapsed early and even its $\min_t \phi_t$ form was hacked when used without outcome reward; PROF (2509.03403) saw entropy collapse; and a 2026 study (2603.06621) watched PRM reward saturate within $100$ steps while AIME accuracy stayed at $0\text{–}4\%$. The only variants that survive bound or telescope the proxy (clipped delta-PRM reached $31.44$ vs. $30.58$ outcome-only in 2410.15115; implicit token-level redistribution in PRIME, 2502.01456; prover advantage in PAV, 2410.08146), and their asymptotic gains over outcome reward are within noise.
  • On-policy Monte Carlo / tree rollouts buy step efficiency, but gains shrink with context length and scale B efficiency · D ceiling. Estimating intermediate state values by branching rollouts and checking completions with the true verifier (VinePPO, 2410.01679; SPO, 2505.23564; TreeRL, 2506.11902) is unbiased and converges in $2\text{–}3\times$ fewer optimizer steps (at higher rollout cost per step). However, standard group sampling already performs implicit Monte Carlo credit assignment across shared prefixes (2509.21154), and explicit tree-credit margins vanish as context and model size grow: SPO's MATH-500 margin over GRPO drops from $+11.6$ pts at $2\text{k}$ context to $+7.6$ at $4\text{k}$ and $+0.8$ at $32\text{k}$ (where AIME24 ties), and TreeRPO's gain drops from $\sim 3$ pts at 1.5B to $<1$ pt at 7B. DeepSeek-R1 (2501.12948v1) and Kimi k1.5 (2501.12599) both abandoned training-time PRMs, reserving PRMs for test-time best-of-$N$ reranking (Lightman et al., 2305.20050) B.

Family 3 · Agentic multi-turn RL

In multi-turn tool-use, coding, and web-browsing agents, the trajectory interleaves policy actions with environment observations. Once the correctness constraints of Chapter 4 are enforced — masking environment tokens (Search-R1, 2503.09516: $0.343 \to 0.431$ EM on 7B) B, preserving exact sampled token IDs (GLM-5, 2602.15763) B, and filtering malformed or timed-out turns (SimpleTIR, 2509.02479: best AIME24 $20.8 \to 50.5$) C — algorithmic refinements play a secondary role to environment and context engineering:

  • State-grouped and turn-level credit. GiGPO (2505.10978) groups repeated environment states across trajectories to form a per-turn baseline, beating GRPO across three seeds on ALFWorld and WebShop B. However, exact state recurrence is common in finite text games and rare in open software engineering or web search, where GiGPO reduces to trajectory-level GRPO. Meanwhile, token- vs. turn-level ratio rankings contradict each other across studies (LOOP, 2502.01600 vs. SORL, 2511.20718) D.
  • Environment quality and test-time context management dominate A. Replacing a weak retriever with a dense retriever doubled exact match in search RL (2505.15117); warm-starting on real rather than synthetic tool trajectories decided whether DemyAgent learned at all (2510.11701); and test-time context management (summarizing or pruning old turns when context fills) independently raised BrowseComp scores by $+14\text{–}16$ points across three frontier labs: DeepSeek-V3.2 ($51.4 \to 67.6$), Kimi K2.5 ($60.6 \to 74.9$), and GLM-5 ($62.0 \to 75.9$).

Family 4 · On-policy distillation (OPD): dense teacher signal that expands coverage

What if we keep the on-policy sampling loop $y \sim \pi_{\text{student}}(\cdot\mid x)$ (eliminating SFT's exposure bias; GKD, 2306.13649) but replace the sparse 1-bit outcome verifier with a stronger teacher policy $\pi_{\text{teacher}}$? Minimizing the mode-seeking divergence $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{student}} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{teacher}})$ yields a policy gradient where every token receives a dense, unhackable advantage (MiniLLM, 2306.08543; Thinking Machines, 2025):

$$\hat{A}_t^{\text{OPD}}(x, y_{\le t}) \;=\; \log \pi_{\text{teacher}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log \pi_{\text{student}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$$

Unlike outcome-reward RL — which supplies one bit at token $T$ and sharpens within the student's existing support — the teacher's per-token log-probabilities tell the student which intermediate tokens to raise even on trajectories that fail at the end. In Qwen3's technical report (2505.09388), on-policy distillation of an 8B model reached $74.4$ on AIME24 using roughly one-tenth the GPU-hours that outcome-reward RL needed to reach $67.6$, while also lifting pass@64 coverage (which RL did not). Thinking Machines reported reaching $70\%$ on AIME24 from a $60\%$ SFT start at $9\text{–}30\times$ less compute than continued SFT. By 2026, this established a clear division of labour at the frontier: RL trains domain specialists at the capability boundary, and on-policy distillation consolidates those specialists into general models — as in DeepSeek-V4, whose final stage replaces mixed RL entirely with full-vocabulary $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{student}} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{teacher}})$ distillation from more than ten RL-trained specialists (2606.19348).

  • Explicit handling of train–inference mismatch (Gap 1) has become standard since the second half of 2025: routing replay and masks (V3.2), an FP32 LM head (M1), recorded inference probabilities (K2.5), truncated importance sampling (OLMo 3), low-precision rollout designed in (V4). This matches Chapter 4.2.
  • Difficulty filtering or removal of zero-variance groups ($p_G \in \{0, 1\}$) in essentially every report that describes its data, and length control in the reward or budget in most.

What is ablated vs. what is declared

Six reports ablate some of their RL algorithm choices: Magistral most thoroughly (batch and minibatch size, advantage normalization, reward granularity, entropy bonus against clip-higher), MiniMax-M1 (its loss against DAPO's and GRPO's), GLM-4.5 (aggregation, length schedule, and curriculum on a smaller proxy model), Kimi k1.5 (negative gradients, curriculum), Seed1.5-Thinking (VAPO against DAPO), and DeepSeek-R1 (PPO against GRPO on a smaller model). Three ablate one component or only systems choices. Four—DeepSeek-V3.2 and V4, Kimi K2, MiniMax-M2—ablate none of their RL algorithm choices; V3.2's stability fixes in particular rest on qualitative statements. The shared choices are mostly the ones with good independent evidence; the contested choices are mostly the ones each lab makes differently without publishing controlled ablations.

The drift toward on-policy distillation

One architectural trend cuts across the table. DeepSeek-V4's final stage replaces mixed RL entirely with on-policy distillation from more than ten domain specialists, each trained with SFT and then GRPO. Qwen3 trains its smaller models by on-policy distillation from larger ones, GLM-4.5 finishes with self-distillation from expert RL models, and V3.2 distills specialists before its final mixed RL stage. RL still produces the domain specialists, but consolidating many skills into one generalist model is increasingly done by on-policy distillation (Chapter 8.4). That fits Chapter 1: RL sharpens within a specialist's support, while distillation transfers support across models with dense per-token supervision.

Every mechanism from Chapter 5 is visible here in twelve lines:

  • Lines 13–15 (PPO-clip & Clip-higher): Computes $-\min(r\hat{A}, \text{clip}(r, 1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}}, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}})\hat{A})$ as a maximum of negatives.   • Lines 16–18 (Dual-clip in Quadrant III): Applies an upper cap $c$ on the negative-advantage loss only where $\hat{A} < 0$ and $r > c$, preventing unbounded gradient explosions when a bad token's probability has risen sharply since rollout.
  • Lines 19–20 (Gap 1 engine-mismatch correction): Multiplies by rollout_is_weights ($\rho^{\text{roll}} = \pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{train}} / \pi_{\text{old}}^{\text{roll}}$)—the only line in the function that can see Gap 1.

9.3 Twelve Algorithmic Pitfalls (踩坑录)

System bugs crash runs; algorithmic pitfalls leave training running and the loss curve looking plausible while silently changing the mathematical objective being optimized. Each pitfall below is documented from local checkouts of open-source RL frameworks (May 2026) and published post-mortems.

  1. A KL loss that does nothing ($k_1$ outside the log-prob). In slime, enabling --use-kl-loss without changing --kl-loss-type uses the naive $k_1 = \log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ estimator as a Differentiable loss term, whose expected gradient is identically zero (Chapter 4.3). No warning is printed. Detect: verify the KL estimator type and confirm measured KL responds to $\beta$.
  2. A KL loss with the wrong tail direction ($k_3$ vs. $k_1$-in-reward). SkyRL enables a $k_3$ KL loss by default, and verl uses $k_3$ when use_kl_loss is enabled. Its expected gradient is that of forward KL $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{ref}} \| \pi_\theta)$, not the reverse KL $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$ named in the RL objective; the two diverge sharply whenever $\pi_\theta$ drifts far from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Detect: check whether KL enters as a detached reward penalty or a $k_3$ loss term.
  3. "CISPO" that is not CISPO. verl's cispo mode reuses the general clip settings, which default to $0.2$ on both sides, clipping the importance weight to $[0.8, 1.2]$. MiniMax-M1 disables the lower bound entirely, and verl's own example script sets clip_ratio_low = 10 for this reason. Running loss_mode=cispo with default clip flags tests a different algorithm. Detect: print the effective clip interval $[1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}}, 1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}}]$.
  4. Hidden off-policy updates in "synchronous" runs. verl's stock batch sizes (train_batch_size=1024, ppo_mini_batch_size=256) take $\mu = 4$ optimizer steps per rollout batch, so "synchronous" training is off-policy from micro-step 2 onward (the $r \equiv 1$ identity). Detect: compute $\mu = (\text{train\_batch\_size} / \text{ppo\_mini\_batch\_size}) \times \text{ppo\_epochs}$ and log the clip fraction.
  5. "Token-level" means three different denominators. verl's token-mean normalizes per mini-batch across workers; DAPO's formula normalizes per prompt group; OLMo 3's text says "across the batch" while its equation normalizes per group. Prompts with different typical response lengths get different relative weights under each. Detect: read the denominator in code, not the mode's string name.
  6. A length-dependent advantage shift that activates zero-variance groups. In OpenRLHF's reinforce_baseline, advantages are first centered within each prompt group and then re-normalized with a mean and variance weighted by token count over the whole batch. When correct and incorrect responses differ in length, the token-weighted batch mean is nonzero, so subtracting it shifts every advantage—and groups where all $G$ responses got the exact same reward (which should have zero gradient) acquire a nonzero gradient proportional to their length. Detect: assert that advantages sum to zero within each prompt group after all normalization steps.
  7. A summed preference loss that should be length-averaged. TRL's IPO implementation once summed token log-probabilities where the paper averages them. Once fixed, IPO performed on par with DPO; before the fix it looked worse (Hugging Face, 2024). Detect: check one batch by hand against the paper's equation.
  8. Infrastructure bugs that depress published baselines. A 2026 audit reports an optimizer-offload bug that silently dropped micro-batches and a loss-aggregation bug that together depressed the supervised baselines of several mixed-policy RL papers; once fixed, plain SFT followed by standard RL beat the proposed methods (arXiv:2604.23747). Detect: reproduce a published baseline score before trusting a delta against it.
  9. Re-tokenized multi-turn conversations. Re-applying a chat template to decoded strings does not reproduce the exact token IDs the policy sampled at rollout time. verl reports this made multi-turn PPO fail to converge (Chapter 8.3). Detect: pass token IDs directly from sampler to trainer and assert token-ID equality.
  10. An inference kernel change that masquerades as an algorithm failure. An attention optimization in vLLM on A100 GPUs raised the sampler–trainer KL ($\text{Gap 1}$) by two orders of magnitude, causing runs to collapse on A100s while recovering when resumed on H100s (Chapter 4.2). Detect: log per-step $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\text{roll}} \| \pi^{\text{train}})$ and investigate hardware/kernel spikes before changing the RL estimator.
  11. Configuration YAML and Python dataclass defaults that disagree. verl's rollout-correction YAML sets importance sampling to null, while the underlying Python dataclass defaults it to sequence-level. Which setting wins depends on how the config is instantiated. Detect: dump and archive the fully resolved configuration object at job startup.
  12. Evaluation settings that move benchmark scores by more than the treatment effect. Temperature, top-$p$, vLLM version, GPU architecture, and prompt wrapper each move AIME scores by 2–6 points (Chapter 3.1). Detect: lock evaluation hyperparameters and run multi-seed pass@$k$ evaluations inside the same harness for baseline and treatment.
The common thread. Every item above leaves training running and the loss curve plausible while altering the gradient's expectation or the evaluation ruler. The defense is always the same: dump the resolved config, inspect the denominator of every average, verify $\sum_i \hat{A}_i = 0$ per group, and log both Gap 1 ($\pi^{\text{roll}}$ vs. $\pi^{\text{train}}$) and Gap 2 ($\pi_{\text{old}}$ vs. $\pi_\theta$) KL divergences from step 1.

10. Synthesis: Six Laws, Training Dynamics Diagnostic Atlas & Decision Guide

Chapters 1 through 8 evaluated design choices across the five axes and four neighbouring families. Read together, nearly every result reduces to six compact algebraic laws. Knowing these six identities makes most new post-training RL papers predictable before reading their benchmark tables.

10.1 Six Algebraic Laws of LLM Post-Training

  1. Law 1 — The On-Policy Collapse ($r \equiv 1$ Identity, Chapter 2.2): $$r_{i,t} \equiv 1 \implies w(r_{i,t}) \cdot m(r_{i,t}, \hat{A}_{i,t}) = 1 \implies \hat{g}(\theta) = \sum_{i,t} c_{i,t} \hat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$$ PPO-clip, clip-higher, CISPO, GSPO, GMPO, and SAPO have the exact same gradient when each rollout batch receives one optimizer step ($\mu = 1$) and $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is recomputed on the trainer. Their differences live entirely in how they treat off-policy ratios $r \ne 1$. Diagnostic rule: before crediting a surrogate loss with a benchmark gain, check how many steps per batch ($\mu$) and how much asynchronous lag ($\tau$) the experiment used.
  2. Law 2 — Prompt Weighting as an Implicit Objective (Chapter 6.1): $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}(\theta)] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{B}} w(p_x) \, \nabla_\theta p_x(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \sum_{x \in \mathcal{B}} F(p_x(\theta)), \qquad F'(p) = w(p) = \frac{1}{s(p)}$$ Dividing binary rewards by a prompt-dependent scale $s(p_x)$ does not improve an estimator of expected reward; it switches the objective to $F(p_x)$. Mean-only subtraction ($s = 1$) optimizes $\sum p_x$; GRPO's $\div\;\text{std}_G$ ($s = \sqrt{p(1-p)}$) optimizes $\sum 2\arcsin\sqrt{p_x}$ (symmetrically over-weighting $p \to 0$ and $p \to 1$); $\text{MaxRL}$ ($s = p$) optimizes $\sum \log p_x$; pass@$k$ optimizes $\sum (1 - (1-p_x)^k)$. Diagnostic rule: ask of any new advantage normalization rule which $F(p)$ it implies, and whether up-weighting $p \to 1$ prompts is desirable.
  3. Law 3 — Sharpening Helps Whenever the Mode Beats the Mean (Chapter 1.2 & Case Study 1): $$\text{pass@1 after pure sharpening} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{\arg\max_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \in \mathcal{Y}^*(x)\}\right] = \text{maj@}\infty(\pi_{\text{ref}})$$ Any update that concentrates probability mass on the current mode—entropy minimization, majority-vote self-reward, format-only rewards, or asymmetric clipping—moves pass@1 from $\mathbb{E}[p_x]$ toward the base model's majority-vote accuracy without requiring any ground-truth correctness signal. Diagnostic rule: the gap between $\text{maj@}k(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ and $\text{pass@1}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is the upper bound on what a label-free or spurious-reward method can achieve. A gain inside that gap on a pretrained-contaminated model (like Qwen2.5-Math) is not evidence of reasoning acquisition.
  4. Law 4 — Quadrant III ($\hat{A} < 0$) Carries Unbounded Variance and Logit-Norm Drift (Chapter 5.1): $$\hat{A}_{i,t} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}) \le 0 \;\text{is bounded above for } \hat{A} > 0, \quad \text{but } -|\hat{A}_{i,t}| \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}) \to +\infty \;\text{as } \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}) \to 0$$ Pushing an improbable negative-advantage token toward zero probability has no upper bound in the objective and inflates unnormalized logit norms across the vocabulary. Off-policy data amplifies this instability in Quadrant III ($\hat{A} < 0$, $r \ll 1$ or $r \gg 1$), which is why PPO's lower clip, dual-clip, TOPR, M2PO, and DeepSeek-V3.2's off-policy sequence mask all target negative-advantage drift specifically. Diagnostic rule: inspect every new surrogate's behavior in the $\hat{A} < 0$ quadrants.
  5. Law 5 — Learned Step-Level Proxies Must Telescope or Be Bounded (Chapter 8.2): $$R'(y) = R(y) + \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \phi_t \;\;\text{preserves } \pi^* \text{ if } \phi_t = \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t) \;\text{(telescoping)}, \quad \text{and is hacked if } \phi_t \text{ is an unconstrained learned score}$$ Summing unconstrained learned per-step scores into the return invites the policy to inflate length, repeat confident-looking boilerplate, or exploit classifier blind spots. Only telescoping potential-based shaping (Ng et al., 1999), min-form aggregation, or filtering/reranking survive optimization pressure.
  6. Law 6 — Data Provenance Outweighs Loss Shape (Chapter 8.1): In preference optimization and distillation, all well-tuned loss shapes (DPO, IPO, SimPO, KTO, WPO) tie within noise once temperature $\beta$ is swept on fixed offline data (+1–5 points), whereas refreshing the preference pairs on-policy from the current iterate $\pi_\theta$ adds +15–20 points. Diagnostic rule: when two methods differ in both data provenance (offline vs. on-policy) and loss formula, attribute the gain to the data provenance until proven otherwise.

The overarching synthesis: Laws 1 and 4 show that the surrogate only acts off-policy, and primarily to prevent negative-advantage collapse. Law 2 shows that normalization chooses an implicit prompt-weighting objective rather than fixing an estimator. Laws 3 and 5 show that a large fraction of reported benchmark gains come from prior sharpening or hackable proxies rather than true exploration. And Law 6 shows that on-policy data provenance dominates loss algebra. Together, they move the central question of LLM RL from "which named algorithm should I use?" to "is my gradient estimator unbiased on the distribution that actually generated the tokens?"—the exact conclusion Engstrom et al. (arXiv:2005.12729) reached for deep RL in 2020.

6. Proxy / Verifier Hacking
Training reward $\bar{R}(t) \uparrow$ monotonically toward $1.0$, while held-out benchmark accuracy peaks and then plunges		 Law I & Law V (Goodhart Exponential Tilt / Non-Telescoping PRM): As $\beta_{\text{eff}} = (\eta K)^{-1} \to 0$, $\pi^*$ concentrates exponentially on a verifier false-positive mode (`y7`) or inflates non-telescoping step scores $\sum_t \phi_t$. Inspect top-rewarded completions with low length or high step repetition; audit verifier on empty/symbolic strings. Patch parser false positives; drop guessable/multiple-choice items; replace additive PRMs with telescoping potentials $\Phi(s_{t+1})-\Phi(s_t)$; or restore an anchor KL penalty ($\beta > 0$ via $k_2$-loss or $k_1$-in-reward).
7. Vanishing Gradient / Step-Rate Stall
Effective gradient norm shrinks over training; $>60\%$ of rollout groups have all-$0$ or all-$1$ rewards		 Law II (Zero-Bit Bernoulli Dead Zones): As prompts polarize to $p_x \in \{0, 1\}$, $I(R;Y\mid x) = H_{\text{Ber}}(p_x) = 0\text{ bits}$. Dead groups contribute $0$ numerator gradient while inflating the `token-mean` denominator. Log the fraction of prompts per batch with $\sum_{i=1}^G R_i \in \{0, G\}$. Filter zero-variance groups ($0 < \sum R_i < G$) or drop solved prompts ($p_x > 0.9$); use a constant denominator $M$ so dead groups do not shrink active prompts' step sizes.
8. Capability Ceiling Stall ($p_x \equiv 0$ on Hard Set)
Easy prompts saturate at $p_x \to 1$, but hard reasoning benchmarks remain flat across thousands of steps		 Theorem 1 (Missing Atomic Motifs in $\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$): Base model lacks atomic verification/backtracking transitions ($p_{\text{step}} \approx 0$), so $G$ rollouts never sample a single positive bridge trajectory. Measure base model $\text{pass@128}$ and count verification/backtracking tokens in initial rollouts. Do not tune the RL loss: inject atomic motifs upstream via SFT priming on verification traces or On-Policy Distillation (OPD) from a stronger specialist teacher, then resume RLVR with larger $L_{\max}$ and batch size $B$.

10.3 The Five-Link Compute Scaling Chain

Using ScaleRL's sigmoid parameterization from Chapter 3.4 (Figure 5)—separating the asymptotic ceiling $A$ from the compute efficiency slope $B$ and midpoint $C_{\text{mid}}$—we can rank the five links of the RL post-training chain by how they scale with compute:

  1. Base model & mid-training (sets ceiling $A$, upstream of RL): Determines the support $\text{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ and initial pass@$k$ reach (Chapter 1.3). No downstream RL loss modification overcomes a missing reasoning primitive in the base checkpoint.
  2. Generation length budget $L_{\max}$ & global batch size (moves ceiling $A$ inside RL): In ScaleRL, expanding $L_{\max}$ from 14k to 32k tokens raised $A$ by $+0.035$ and scaling batch size from 512 to 2,048 prompts raised $A$ by $+0.040$, at the cost of $4.4\times$ more compute to reach midpoint $C_{\text{mid}}$ (Chapter 7.1).
  3. Numerical consistency & MoE routing replay (decides whether the run survives to $A$): Without Gap 1 corrections and routing replay (R3 / Keep Routing), multi-step and MoE runs suffer late-stage collapse after thousands of GPU-hours, fitting as an artificially depressed ceiling (Chapter 4.2).
  4. Surrogate loss, normalization, aggregation & curriculum filtering (moves efficiency $B$, not ceiling $A$): In leave-one-out ablations, CISPO, batch std normalization, prompt-level averaging, and zero-variance filtering accelerate convergence along the same sigmoid curve without raising the asymptote $A$.
  5. Staleness $\tau$ & step size $\eta$ (joint stability budget): Maximum stable learning rate scales inversely with pipeline lag ($\eta_{\max} \propto (\mu + \tau)^{-1}$), trading hardware utilization against per-sample gradient fidelity (Chapter 5.2).
  • Choose by efficiency $B$ at small scale, and by stability at large scale: Efficiency differences appear cheaply in the first few hundred steps; numerical and off-policy stability differences appear late and determine whether the run reaches its asymptote.
  • Monitor the response truncation rate as an early-warning indicator: Truncation rates climbing into the 10–15% band reliably precede length collapse and gradient instability.
  • Expect recipe rankings to cross over with compute: As shown in Figure 5, a recipe that raises the ceiling $A$ (such as a 32k length budget or larger batch size) often trails a fast-sharpening baseline at small compute budgets before overtaking it late.

10.3 A Practitioner's Decision Guide: What to Set, in What Order

Translating our Category I–III verdicts into a concrete configuration procedure yields a four-tier hierarchy ordered by strength of evidence:

1 · Get Right First (Category I Correctness)

Non-negotiable gradient & baseline hygiene

• Keep the estimator honest: $\mu = 1$ step per batch where affordable A, or bounded staleness with sampler-aware IS/masking when $\mu > 1$ B.
• On MoE models with $\mu > 1$, enable routing replay (R3 / Keep Routing) A.
• Fix Gap 1 at the source (FP32 LM head, matched kernels) and log $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\text{roll}} \| \pi^{\text{train}})$ from step 1 B.
• If using a critic, set $\lambda = 1.0$ A and do not initialize from the reward model B.
• For multi-turn agents, mask environment tokens and pass raw sampled token IDs B.
• Audit verifier precision against empty/short/boilerplate completions B.

2 · Cheap, Neutral Defaults

Start every run here before adding complexity

• Baseline: Group-mean subtraction (RLOO / Dr. GRPO) without group standard-deviation division ($\div\;\text{std}_G$ off).
• Aggregation: Prompt-level sum divided by a constant $M$ or batch-token mean—no $1/|y_i|$ length penalty.
• KL & Entropy: $\beta = 0$ (no static KL anchor) for verifiable rewards; keep detached $k_1$-in-reward or $k_3$ loss only for learned reward models. Entropy bonus $= 0$, rollout temperature $T = 1.0$.
• Data filtering: Drop zero-variance prompt groups ($p_G \in \{0, 1\}$) and solved prompts.

3 · Tune on Your Own Model & Budget

Context-dependent knobs that flip sign across setups

• Upper clip bound $\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$: Tune in $[0.20, 0.28]$ when $\mu > 1$, especially on instruction-tuned checkpoints.
• Truncated responses & length budget $L_{\max}$: Tune soft overlong penalties and mask-truncated-rollout flags jointly with $L_{\max}$.
• Batch size & group size $G$: Scale batch size and $L_{\max}$ when targeting a higher asymptote $A$; increase $G \in \{16, 32, 64\}$ or apply $\text{MaxRL}$ weighting when targeting hard prompts.
• Preference learning: Make preference pairs on-policy before tuning the loss shape; then sweep $\beta$ on DPO / SimPO + NLL.

4. What to skip unless you can ablate it at $\ge 3$ seeds

  • Swapping one clipped surrogate for another (CISPO, GSPO, GMPO, SAPO) in an on-policy ($\mu = 1$) run: by the $r \equiv 1$ identity they compute the exact same gradient.
  • Training a learned critic for single-turn verifiable reasoning: doubles memory and compute without raising the asymptotic pass@1 ceiling.
  • Summing a learned Process Reward Model (PRM) directly into the RL return: consistently reward-hacked unless constrained to telescoping potentials, min-aggregation, or best-of-$N$ reranking.
  • Complex turn-level credit assignment in non-branching environments: episode-level outcome advantage with masked environment tokens matches turn-level schemes until the trajectory graph explicitly branches.

10.4 The 12-Question Verification Checklist

Before trusting a published RL algorithm claim—or an internal ablation—verify these twelve questions, ordered by how frequently the answer reverses the conclusion in the 2025–2026 literature:

  1. Which base models were tested? If only Qwen2.5 or Qwen2.5-Math, treat the result as a hypothesis about that model family (Case Study 1: random rewards add +21 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B and hurt Llama/OLMo).
  2. How many training seeds and how many evaluation rollouts ($K$) per problem? Single-rollout AIME-24 ($N=30, K=1$) has a $\sim 26$-point minimum detectable difference; $K \ge 32$ across $\ge 3$ training seeds is required to resolve 2–3 point gains (Chapter 3.1).
  3. Was the baseline tuned with equal effort in the exact same codebase? Check for crippled baselines ($\lambda = 0.95$ without critic warmup, untuned $\beta$, or infrastructural offload bugs; Case Study 4).
  4. What were $\mu$ (steps per batch), $\tau$ (async lag), and how was $\pi_{\text{old}}$ computed? If $\mu = 1$ and $\pi_{\text{old}}$ was recomputed on the trainer, $r \equiv 1$ and any claimed surrogate improvement is a measurement artifact (Chapter 2.2).
  5. Was a random-reward or format-only control included? Any pass@1 gain inside the base model's $\text{maj@}k - \text{pass@1}$ gap could come from blind distribution sharpening (Law 3).
  6. Is the evaluation benchmark temporal-leakage clean? Check release dates and partial-prompt completion rates (Chapter 1.3).
  7. Is the x-axis wall-clock GPU-hours / total tokens or optimizer steps? Curriculum filtering, large group sizes $G$, and dense tree rollouts spend $2\text{–}6\times$ more generation compute per optimizer step (Chapter 3.3).
  8. Are reported scores peak-in-window or final checkpoint? Reporting the best checkpoint along a noisy trajectory creates positive selection bias and hides late collapse (Case Study 2).
  9. Is the ablation cumulative (forward) or leave-one-out (backward)? Overlapping stabilizers look decisive when added first in a forward chain and redundant in a leave-one-out study (Case Study 3).
  10. Are both pass@1 and pass@$k$ ($k \ge 16$) reported? Standard RL raises pass@1 by sharpening while often flatlining or reducing pass@$k$ coverage (Chapter 1.2).
  11. Was the starting checkpoint a raw pretrained base or an SFT/instruction-tuned model? Hyperparameter effects (such as $\varepsilon_{\text{high}}$ and length aggregation) frequently flip sign between base and instruct checkpoints.
  12. Does the code's resolved config match the paper's equation? Check the twelve silent pitfalls in Chapter 9.3.

10.5 From Single-Benchmark RLVR to Frontier Post-Training Systems: Five Real-World Battlegrounds Beyond the Coordinate Table

The Two-Layer Coordinate System ($\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}$) resolves the three-year academic debate over single-domain math/code RLVR and preference loss functions—making it an effective filter for eliminating redundant loss-of-the-week ablations and debugging off-policy or MoE infrastructure bugs. However, from the vantage point of a post-training lead at a frontier laboratory (such as OpenAI or Anthropic), $95\%$ of the published literature operates inside a narrow bubble: training a 7B–32B model for 200–500 steps on a single math benchmark and reporting Pass@1 on AIME. Shipping a frontier generalist model requires simultaneously aligning math, competitive programming, repository-scale software engineering (SWE / bash / computer use), long-form writing, multi-hop research, and safety/honesty inside a single model. In that production regime, five system-level battlegrounds dominate day-to-day R&D:

  1. Multi-Domain Gradient Interference & The Alignment Tax (Vector $\vec{R}$, Not Scalar $R$): In single-domain papers, A1 is a single reward channel and A2 ($w(p_x)$) weights difficulty within one homogeneous dataset. In frontier production, a single training stage mixes heterogeneous environments: $32\text{k}$-token binary-verifier math and code unit tests alongside $1\text{k}$-token continuous neural RM/rubric judges for prose quality, tone, instruction following, and safety refusals. Because exact verifiers and soft RMs have vastly different gradient variance profiles, sequence lengths, and optimal KL step sizes, naive joint training causes cross-domain cannibalization (long-CoT RL degrades prose style, induces over-thinking on trivial queries, or hurts calibration; conversely, chat RM updates lobotomize deep reasoning). Here, the real A2 design problem is cross-environment gradient norm balancing, per-domain KL budgets $\beta_{\text{domain}}$, explicit thinking-budget conditioning, or training domain-specialist RL teachers followed by unified On-Policy Distillation (OPD) into a single generalist student.
  2. Grading & Environment Engineering Against an Adversarial Optimizer (A1 Is $80\%$ of R&D, C1 Is $5\%$): While academic surveys devote most of their pages to surrogate clipping (C1) and baseline algebra (C2), over $80\%$ of frontier post-training headcount is spent on environments and grading signals (A1). On open-ended professional tasks (writing a full pull request, synthesizing a multi-source research report, medical/legal analysis), binary ground-truth verifiers do not exist. Moreover, the static false-positive formula $\mathbb{E}[\hat{R}] = (1-\alpha_{\text{FP}}-\alpha_{\text{FN}})p + \alpha_{\text{FP}}$ understates Goodhart risk: an RL policy actively searches for adversarial completions where $\alpha_{\text{FP}}(y) \to 1$ as $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$ grows. Frontier defenses rely not on cranking up $\beta$, but on decomposed structured rubrics, reference-anchored pairwise generative judges (Critique-then-Judge), pessimistic RM ensembles ($\min_k R_k$), explicit length/style neutralization inside the reward, and iterative online RM + policy co-evolution.
  3. Long-Horizon Stateful Agents ($50\text{–}150$ Irreversible Tool Turns) vs. Single-Turn In-Context CoT (C2 Cannot Be Extrapolated Blindly): Controlled studies on single-turn math (GenAC at $\sim 200$ steps; SPO at $32\text{k}$ context) show critic-free group baselines tying learned critics and tree credit shrinking to $+0.8$ points at $32\text{k}$. That equivalence holds for single-turn text CoT because token generation is cheap and the model can backtrack in-context ("Wait, let me re-evaluate"). It cannot be extrapolated to $50\text{–}150$ turn stateful container agents (SWE-bench, Computer Use, deep web browsing): when a trajectory spans $80$ bash executions over $30$ minutes and $100\text{k}$ tokens, environment transitions are slow and irreversible, brute-force $G=64$ group rollouts per task are prohibitively expensive, and broadcasting a single $1\text{-bit}$ outcome across $80$ tool turns yields near-zero signal-to-noise ratio. In long-horizon stateful agents, turn-level value baselines, state-checkpoint branching (e.g., GiGPO state grouping), and dense step-level environment feedback remain essential.
  4. The Multi-Stage Outer Loop: RL as Frontier Explorer (Producer) $\to$ OPD / Rejection SFT as Entropy-Resetting Compressor (Consumer): Analyzing RL as a single continuous run from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to $\pi^*$ makes support bounds ($\operatorname{supp}(\pi_\theta) \subseteq \operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})$) and single-run entropy collapse (D1) look like terminal walls. Frontier labs never ship a single uninterrupted RL run; they operate a multi-generational outer flywheel: $$\pi_{\text{ref}}^{(k)} \xrightarrow{\text{High-temp / Pass@}k\text{ RL Exploration}} \text{Verified Trajectories} \xrightarrow{\text{OPD / Rejection SFT + Context Reset}} \pi_{\text{ref}}^{(k+1)} \xrightarrow{\text{Generation } k+1\text{ RL}}$$ In this outer loop, aggressive low-$\beta$ or pass@$k$-weighted RL is used as a high-compute search probe (Producer) to uncover hard solutions even if the policy's entropy collapses or its formatting drifts; On-Policy Distillation (OPD) and Rejection-Sampled SFT then act as the compressor (Consumer) that distills those discovered trajectories back into a fresh, high-entropy checkpoint $\pi_{\text{ref}}^{(k+1)}$ (or mid-training mix) for the next generation.
  5. Dynamic Compute Allocation Across the Pass-Rate Phase Transition (Turning Static Cond into a Dynamic Controller): Treating Cond ($P(p_x)$, group size $G$, batch size $B$) as static background misses the central resource-allocation problem of large-scale RL. Early in training, most prompts sit in the high-variance zone $p_x \in [0.2, 0.8]$, so a small static group size ($G=8$) yields high active token yield (D2). Late in training, $P_k(p_x)$ bifurcates: $90\%$ of prompts saturate at $p_x \to 1$ while frontier problems sit at $p_x < 0.01$. Keeping a fixed $G=8$ causes almost every group to return all-$1$ or all-$0$ rewards ($\hat{A} \equiv 0$, D2 $\to 0$). Compute-optimal training requires dynamically evicting mastered prompts ($p_x \to 1$), scaling rollout budget $G(x) \propto 1/\hat{p}_x$ on frontier prompts, and recognizing when persistent $\hat{p}_x \equiv 0$ hits the base-model support wall (`y9`)—where marginal GPU hours must be shifted from RL back to mid-training data acquisition.

11.2 Eight Open Mathematical Questions

Each question below marks a point where a rigorous theorem or tight statistical bound would settle an empirical debate that current papers can only grade heuristically:

  1. Identifiability of the compute asymptote $A$: Sigmoid and unbounded log-linear curves fit finite RL training trajectories nearly equally well (arXiv:2610.04158). Under what conditions on the prompt difficulty distribution is the asymptote $A$ statistically identifiable from compute budget $C$, and how wide is its minimax confidence interval?
  2. Tight bias bounds for length-normalized importance filtering: Token-level truncated IS corrects per-step conditionals without correcting prefix state visitation, incurring bias that grows polynomially in sequence length $|y|$ (Chapter 4.2). Length-normalized geometric filters ($\text{GSPO}, \text{GMPO}$) stabilize long-horizon training empirically without a matching bias-variance bound. What is the exact bias of geometric-mean ratio clipping as a function of $|y|$ and per-token Rényi divergence?
  3. First-principles derivation of the staleness $\times$ step-size stability law: Empirically, $\eta_{\max} \propto (\mu + \tau)^{-1}$ (Chapter 5.2). Proving a tight lower bound showing that mirror descent with stale gradients diverges whenever $(\mu + \tau)\eta$ exceeds a curvature-dependent threshold would turn a tuning heuristic into a formal system-design law.
  4. Optimal prompt-weighting transform $F(p)$ across training time: Every advantage normalization ascends $\sum_x F(p_x)$ (Law 2). Given an initial pass-rate distribution $\rho_0(p)$ across prompts, what schedule of transforms $F_t(p)$ maximizes final pass@1—or pass@$k$—under a fixed rollout budget?
  5. Cross-prompt generalization and support expansion: On a single prompt, outcome-reward updates only reweight existing support (Layer 1). New capabilities must arise from cross-prompt parameter sharing during gradient updates. Can we bound how much probability mass leaks onto novel reasoning chains on prompt $x'$ when updating on prompt $x$ via neural tangent or feature-learning kernels?
  6. Tight upper bounds on label-free sharpening: Updates that concentrate mass push pass@1 toward $\text{maj@}\infty(\pi_{\text{ref}})$ (Law 3). How closely do entropy minimization and asymmetric clipped random-reward updates approach this theoretical limit, and at what rate?
  7. Safe intermediate credit between potential shaping and free learned scores: Potential-based shaping $\phi_t = \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)$ preserves $\pi^*$ exactly, while unconstrained additive PRMs are exploited (Law 5). Which bounded or min-aggregated step-reward operators guarantee $\varepsilon$-preservation of the ground-truth optimal policy set?
  8. Multi-step policy entropy dynamics under clipping and batch reuse: The single-step entropy change equals $-\eta\,\text{Cov}_{\pi_\theta}(\log\pi_\theta, \hat{A})$ (Chapter 7.3). Can multi-step entropy collapse under $\mu > 1$ clipped updates be predicted analytically from initial rollout log-probability variance?
