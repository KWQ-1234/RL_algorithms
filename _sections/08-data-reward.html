7. Levers Outside the Loss: Batch Size, Generation Budget, Filtering, and Verifiers

Steps 1 and 2 of the training loop — which prompts are sampled, how long rollouts may run, and how responses are scored — sit outside the policy-gradient loss function. Yet in controlled compute-scaling studies, these external inputs are the only within-RL knobs that reliably move the pass@1 asymptote $A$, while verifier precision determines whether the target distribution $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R/\beta)$ aligns with truth or collapses into reward hacking.

Boundary Condition Cond · What actually raises the pass@1 ceiling: batch size and generation length

In ScaleRL's sigmoid scaling fits (Case Study 3; 2510.13786), two sampling parameters move the asymptote $A$ well beyond the $\pm 0.02$ repeat-run noise floor B:

  • Rollout batch size. Increasing the batch from $512 \to 768 \to 2{,}048$ prompts raises the fitted asymptote from $A \approx 0.605 \to 0.610 \to 0.645$. Smaller batches appear faster at small compute budgets ($C < C_{\text{mid}}$) and are overtaken later, even though per-step entropy trajectories look nearly identical.
  • Generation length budget. Extending maximum rollout length from $14\text{k}$ to $32\text{k}$ tokens lifts $A$ from $0.610$ to $0.645$, at the cost of a $4.4\times$ increase in the compute needed to reach the midpoint ($C_{\text{mid}}: 2{,}542 \to 11{,}272$). Longer budgets expand the set of multi-step verification and backtracking trajectories the policy is allowed to complete.
  • Group size $G$ at fixed total batch is second-order. Holding total batch tokens fixed while varying $G \in \{8, 16, 24, 32\}$ yields $A \in \{0.585, 0.610, 0.590, 0.595\}$ — a $0.025$ spread at the edge of noise with no monotone trend.

7.2 Information-Theoretic Filtering ($H_{\text{Ber}}(p_x)$) & Truncation Dynamics

Zero-variance filtering as Shannon information maximization B efficiency/stability · D ceiling. For a prompt $x$ with binary pass rate $p_x \in [0, 1]$, each rollout $Y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ carries Shannon mutual information with the verifier outcome $R \in \{0, 1\}$ equal to the binary entropy:

$$I(R;\, Y \mid x) = H_{\text{Ber}}(p_x) = -p_x \log_2 p_x - (1 - p_x)\log_2(1 - p_x) \in [0, 1]\text{ bit}, \qquad \Pr(\text{all } G \text{ equal} \mid p_x) = p_x^G + (1 - p_x)^G$$

When $p_x \in \{0, 1\}$ (or whenever a finite sample of $G$ rollouts yields $r_1 = \dots = r_G$, which occurs with probability $p_x^G + (1-p_x)^G$), the empirical group carries zero bits of contrast information and every advantage $\hat{A}_i$ is identically zero. That group contributes zero numerator gradient, yet under token-mean aggregation its tokens still enter the denominator $\sum_j |y_j|$, randomly attenuating the effective learning rate across batches. Dynamic sampling (DAPO, 2503.14476) and zero-variance filtering discard such $0$-bit groups and refill the batch until $0 < \sum_{i=1}^G r_i < G$, while difficulty curricula drop mastered prompts with $p_x \to 1$ (AceReason, 2505.16400). Filtering maximizes informative gradient steps per update, though refilling carries a rollout rejection cost ($41\%$ of samples survive at $G = 16$ vs. $62\%$ at $G = 512$ in BroRL, 2510.01180), leaves the asymptote $A$ unchanged in ScaleRL's leave-one-out runs, and proved unnecessary when using a constant normalizer $M$ in JustRL (2512.16649) and on Countdown (2512.07611).

Overlong truncation handling and length curricula D. When a rollout hits the maximum token cap without finishing, DAPO masks it out of the loss and adds a soft length penalty ($+6$ and $+3$ in Table 1). Subsequent independent studies split sharply based on the length budget:

  • At a tight budget ($8\text{k}$ tokens), masking truncated responses helps clearly by removing noisy negative gradients on valid reasoning chains that merely ran out of room (Tricks or Traps, 2508.08221).
  • At a generous budget ($20\text{k–}32\text{k}$ tokens), the benefit vanishes or reverses: OLMo 3 found no consistent gain (2512.13961), JustRL found DAPO's soft overlong penalty lowered AIME24 by $\sim 5$ points and collapsed entropy (2512.16649), and Skywork-OR1 warns that masking truncated trajectories at $32\text{k}$ removes the only penalty on runaway rambling — a form of length reward hacking (2505.22312). One diagnostic holds across setups: a truncation rate climbing past $10\text{–}15\%$ reliably precedes training instability (ScaleRL) B.
  • Length curricula (training short then long) split the same way: used in Skywork-OR1 and AceReason, but found irreversibly worse than a single $64\text{k}$ stage in GLM-4.5 (2508.06471) and harmful on Qwen3-4B in Polaris.

Coordinates A1 & A4 · Verifier asymmetry, label-free sharpening, and when anchor KL is load-bearing

Why false positives and false negatives are fundamentally asymmetric B. Suppose a binary verifier has false-negative rate $\alpha = \Pr(\hat{r}=0 \mid r=1)$ and false-positive rate $\beta = \Pr(\hat{r}=1 \mid r=0)$. If those error rates are uniform across responses, the observed pass rate on prompt $x$ is $\tilde{p}_x = (1 - \alpha - \beta)p_x + \beta$, so the expected gradient is simply rescaled:

$$\nabla_\theta \tilde{p}_x \;=\; (1 - \alpha - \beta)\,\nabla_\theta p_x$$

Uniform label noise slows learning without biasing the optimum. In reality, verifier errors are response-dependent, and the two error types behave asymmetrically under $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(R/\beta)$:

  1. False negatives waste signal (an efficiency loss). Rule-based math verifiers achieve $>99\%$ precision but average only $86\%$ recall — and one popular parser recalled just $47\%$ of valid answers on WebInstruct (2505.22203). TinyV found that $38.5\%$ of responses marked wrong in a verified math dataset were actually correct; adding a small neural verifier only as a second pass on rejected answers recovered $+2.6\text{–}2.8$ points (2505.14625).
  2. False positives create an exploitable mode (a ceiling catastrophe). Any response $y_{\text{hack}}$ that fools the verifier into returning $\hat{r} = 1$ (like candidate y7 in Figure 1) becomes a global maximizer of the objective. In 2505.22203, a $1.5\text{B}$ generative verifier used directly as the reward was quickly hacked into accepting a single "{" character or gibberish, collapsing training (whereas a discriminative verifier resisted exploitation). Frontier recipes reflect this law: Kimi k1.5 removes multiple-choice, true/false, proof, and guessable prompts from its RL set (2501.12599), and DeepSeek-R1 avoids neural reward models during reasoning RL (2501.12948).

Label-free rewards obey the sharpening bound B limits · D general. Methods that train without ground-truth labels — entropy minimization (2505.15134), test-time majority-vote rewards (TTRL, 2504.16084), and self-certainty (2505.19590) — confirm Pattern 3: TTRL lifted Mistral-Nemo by $9\text{–}10$ points on AMC and MATH-500 (where its mode is often right) while driving AIME 2024 from $0.8 \to 0$ (where its mode is wrong), and offline self-certainty was hacked by appending already-solved problems to the output.

When to drop vs. keep the KL anchor to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (Coordinate A4) B. Layer 2 of Chapter 1 explains why the role of $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ splits cleanly by verifier type:

  • With a high-precision rule-based verifier, drop the anchor KL to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Because the verifier cannot easily be fooled (`y7` is absent), holding the policy near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ only caps how far $\pi^*$ can sharpen: in Skywork-OR1, setting $\beta = 10^{-3}$ in stage 2 caused AIME24 to plateau, and every released checkpoint uses $\beta = 0$ (2505.22312; see also 2505.22617). DAPO, Magistral, GLM-4.5, MiniMax-M1, ScaleRL, OLMo 3 and JustRL all set $\beta = 0$. Systems that keep a KL term use a proximal reference instead: DeepSeek-R1 resets $\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$ every $400$ steps at $\beta = 0.001$ (2501.12948v2), ProRL resets on validation stalls (2505.24864), and Kimi k1.5 regularizes toward the previous iterate $\pi_k$ (2501.12599) C.
  • With a learned reward model or rubric judge, the anchor KL is mandatory. When $R$ is a neural proxy or LLM judge (Rubrics as Rewards, 2507.17746), true quality follows Gao et al.'s hump-shaped over-optimization law $R_{\text{gold}}(d) \approx d(\alpha - \beta \log d)$ in $d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})}$ (2210.10760; InstructGPT, 2203.02155). In a careful PPO replication, 1B runs that drifted to $\mathrm{KL} \in [50, 85]$ maximized the proxy RM while dropping below a $20\%$ human win rate (2403.17031). Here the KL penalty prevents the policy from reaching the `y7` regime of Figure 1.
