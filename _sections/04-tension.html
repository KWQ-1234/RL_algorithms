Part II · Law V: Statistical Identifiability & Denoising Filters

Deriving the Minimum Detectable Effect ($\text{MDE}_{95\%}$) of finite reasoning benchmarks and the identifiability limits of compute-scaling asymptotes $A$ vs. convergence velocity $B$.

3. Law V · Statistical Identifiability: Noise Floors, Confounders & Why "Ceiling" Claims Fail

The unified estimator of Chapter 2 tells us what a paper changed algebraically; **Law V (Statistical Identifiability)** tells us whether the reported benchmark delta is mathematically distinguishable from noise. In LLM RL, the evaluation ruler is frequently noisier than the $1\text{–}3$ percentage-point treatment effects papers claim to detect. Before grading any algorithmic mechanism, we pass empirical claims through four quantitative filters.

3.1 Filter 1 · The Three-Source Variance Law & Minimum Detectable Effect ($\text{MDE}_{95\%}$)

1. Combined evaluation-sampling and training-seed variance. Suppose a benchmark has $n$ problems, each evaluated with $k$ rollouts across $S$ independent training seeds. If problem $j$ has conditional pass rate $p_j$ and run-to-run training seed variance is $\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2$, the standard error of the difference $\hat{\Delta} = \hat{R}_A - \hat{R}_B$ between two independently trained and evaluated methods is:

$$\sigma_{\Delta} = \sqrt{2\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p_j(1 - p_j)}{k} + \frac{\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2}{S}\right)} \;\ge\; \sqrt{\frac{2\,\bar{\sigma}_{\text{eval}}^2}{n k} + \frac{2\,\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2}{S}}$$

Even if we ignore training-seed variance ($\sigma_{\text{seed}} = 0$) and consider only evaluation sampling on AIME ($n = 30$ problems, $1\text{ problem} = 3.33\text{ points}$), at the binary variance bound $p_j = 1/2$ the $95\%$ Minimum Detectable Effect ($\text{MDE}_{95\%} \approx 1.96\,\sigma_\Delta$) for comparing two runs is:

Samples per problem $k$Std of one score $\sigma$ (pts)Std of difference $\sigma_\Delta$ (pts)$2\sigma_\Delta$ noise band (pts)
$k = 1$ (greedy or single sample)9.112.925.8
$k = 4$4.66.512.9

Even though real models have many problems with $p_j$ near $0$ or $1$, the order of magnitude is unforgiving: a single-sample AIME comparison cannot distinguish methods differing by less than $\sim 20\text{–}26$ points, and even at avg@32 ($k = 32$) increments under $\sim 4.6$ points sit inside the worst-case $2\sigma_\Delta$ evaluation band.

2. Training-seed variance (rarely measured). Two training runs with different random seeds produce different policies, and no amount of evaluation sampling removes that spread. The few studies that measure it find substantial run-to-run variation: ScaleRL's three repeated runs place the fitted asymptote $A$ within $\pm 0.015$ ($1.5$ percentage points) and treat differences under $\pm 0.02$ as noise (2510.13786); LOOP reports $66.4 \pm 4.8$ on AppWorld across three runs (2502.01600); WPO reports its DPO baseline at $20.6 \pm 0.7$ AlpacaEval LC over five seeds (2406.11827). Most 2025 ablation tables run one seed per row.

3. Evaluation configuration drift (systematic). Hochlehnert et al. re-evaluated published reasoning checkpoints and found that varying sampling temperature shifts AIME scores by up to $\sim 15$ points, top-$p$ by up to $\sim 8$, hardware cluster by up to $\sim 8$, and switching evaluation frameworks moved one model by $4.5$ points (2504.07086). Numbers copied from another paper's table are therefore invalid baselines.

Three structural flaws prevent Table 1 from establishing per-component claims: (1) one training run per row with no seed error bars; (2) fixed sequential order, where each increment is conditioned on all preceding rows rather than tested via leave-one-out (unlike VAPO from the same group a month later, which ran 3 seeds and one-at-a-time ablations); and (3) an under-tuned internal starting point ($30$ vs. R1-Zero's $47$). DAPO released a valuable open recipe, but its table proves only that the full bundle reaches $50$ in that setting.

Filters 2–4 · Base models, weak baselines, and compute accounting

  • Filter 2: Base-model priors and contamination. As Case Study 1 established, Qwen2.5-Math gains $+21.4$ on MATH-500 from random rewards and reproduces $54.6\%$ of problem suffixes verbatim. Even within the same family, recipe effects flip sign: on Qwen3-8B, both the better loss-aggregation mode and the benefit of clip-higher reverse between the base model and the instruction-tuned model (Tricks or Traps, 2508.08221).
  • Filter 3: Broken baselines and silent framework bugs. VC-PPO and VAPO measure $+36\text{–}55$ point gains against a vanilla PPO baseline scoring $5\text{–}5.6$ on AIME because that baseline uses $\lambda = 0.95$ and a reward-model-initialized critic (Case Study 4). In preference learning, DPO on Mistral-7B (UltraChat SFT + UltraFeedback) scores $15.1$ AlpacaEval LC in the SimPO paper (2405.14734) and $20.6 \pm 0.7$ in the WPO paper (2406.11827) — a $5.5$-point baseline swing that dwarfs most proposed loss gains. Framework bugs silently depress baselines too: TRL's IPO loss once summed over tokens instead of averaging (Hugging Face, 2024), and a 2026 audit reports in its abstract that an optimizer-offload bug dropping micro-batches plus a loss-aggregation bug depressed the SFT baselines of several mixed-policy papers (2604.23747).
  • Filter 4: Step counting vs. GPU-hours, and peak vs. final checkpoints. Reporting accuracy against optimizer steps flatters methods that do more work per step: PRIME takes $680\text{ s}$ per step versus $531\text{ s}$ ($+28\%$) for its RLOO baseline (2502.01456, Table 2), and TreeRL trains on $480$ responses per update against its baseline's $256$ (2506.11902). Meanwhile, reporting the best checkpoint inside a training window rather than the final checkpoint conceals late entropy or routing collapse.

Why "raises the ceiling" is the hardest claim to establish

Case Study 3 · Ceiling vs. Efficiency

ScaleRL: What sigmoid compute fits reveal — and where they contradict themselves

ScaleRL (2510.13786) is the only large-scale study ($>400\text{k}$ GB200 GPU-hours across an 8B dense model and Llama-4 Scout MoE on Polaris-53K) that explicitly separates a recipe change's effect on the asymptotic ceiling $A$ from its effect on compute efficiency $B, C_{\text{mid}}$ by fitting validation pass rate $R$ against compute $C$:

$$R(C) = R_0 + \frac{A - R_0}{1 + (C_{\text{mid}} / C)^B}$$

What ScaleRL establishes: (1) Across three repeated runs, $A$ varies by $\pm 0.015$, setting a $\pm 0.02$ noise threshold. (2) Two non-loss knobs clearly lift $A$ beyond noise: batch size ($512 \to 2{,}048$ prompts lifts $A$ from $0.605 \to 0.645$) and generation length ($14\text{k} \to 32\text{k}$ tokens lifts $A$ from $0.610 \to 0.645$ at $4.4\times$ higher $C_{\text{mid}}$). (3) In leave-one-out (LOO) ablations starting from the strong final recipe ($16\text{k}$ GPU-hours per run), removing any single algorithmic component leaves the asymptote $A$ within the $\pm 0.02$ noise band; components only move efficiency $B$ (e.g. replacing CISPO with DAPO lowers $B$ from $2.01$ to $1.77$).

The methodological lesson: Forward ablations add a component to a weak baseline that suffers from unmitigated failure modes (e.g. noisy off-policy ratios), whereas LOO removes one component from a mature recipe where overlapping safeguards (such as CISPO and FP32 logits) cover the same weakness. Moreover, $A$ itself is an extrapolation beyond observed compute: a 2026 follow-up found that a log-linear curve $R(C) = a \log C + b$ with no asymptote at all is "often nearly indistinguishable" from the sigmoid over the fitted window (2610.04158, §6.1), and runs that destabilize early get fitted with an artificially low $A$. We therefore apply an asymmetric reading rule throughout: "moves efficiency $B$" is directly observed, whereas differences in $A$ below $0.02\text{–}0.03$ are graded as unresolved.
